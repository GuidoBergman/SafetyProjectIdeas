# Research Question

How many control-eval episodes are actually needed to certify that an untrusted model's sabotage rate is below a target with stated confidence, and how strong is the evidence behind already-published control-eval safety scores?

# Approach Outline

Import acceptance sampling from quality engineering: treat each control episode as a pass/fail (or caught/uncaught) trial, then derive the Operating Characteristic (OC) curve relating sample size and observed failures to the producer's/consumer's risk of accepting an unsafe sabotage rate. Build a small Python tool that, given a target sabotage rate and confidence, outputs the required clean-episode count, and conversely that, given a published result (N episodes, k failures), outputs a confidence interval and the strongest sabotage-rate claim the data supports. Re-analyze numbers from published control evals to show where point estimates over-state the evidence. Everything is analytical/CPU statistics with no model inference, completable in ~30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build and validate the OC-curve tool: implement the binomial acceptance-sampling math and verify it against textbook MIL-STD-105 examples. Measure: required clean-episode count to certify sabotage rate < 1% at 95% confidence. Expected outcome: a reusable calculator showing certification needs hundreds of clean episodes, often more than published evals run.
- Re-analyze published control evals: extract (episodes, failures) from 3-5 public control-eval results and compute exact (Clopper-Pearson) CIs and the OC-implied risk. Measure: width of the CI and gap between the reported point estimate and the conservative bound. Expected outcome: several headline safety scores have CIs wide enough that the safe conclusion is not statistically supported.
- Sample-size guidance table: produce a lookup table of required episodes vs target sabotage rate and confidence for typical control-eval regimes. Measure: episodes needed across a grid of targets. Expected outcome: a practical table evaluators can cite to justify their N, exposing under-powered designs.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If control evals adopt an explicit sample-size/confidence framework, then safety decisions stop relying on noisy point estimates that can read as 'safe' by chance, so deployment gates for untrusted models become statistically honest. Honest evidence thresholds reduce the chance of greenlighting a model whose true sabotage rate is dangerous, directly tightening the epistemics of safety cases that stand between a scheming model and catastrophic deployment.

# Strength Rationale

Maximal counterfactual value and narrow scope: it is statistics-only re-analysis of already-public numbers needing zero compute, training, or proprietary data, yet it strengthens the epistemic foundation of control evals, an area frontier labs rarely formalize publicly. The deliverable, a sample-size calculator plus a re-analysis of published claims, is self-contained with an explicit success criterion (correctly reproduces textbook OC curves and yields valid CIs).

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as power analysis for AI safety evals: rather than acceptance sampling, present it as classic statistical power/sample-size planning, making it portable to any pass/fail safety benchmark, not just control.
