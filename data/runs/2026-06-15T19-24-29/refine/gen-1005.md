# Research Question

Without communicating, do independently developed LLMs converge on the same Schelling (focal) point in coordination games, and do they agree with each other more than humans do? Shared focal points would let separate models tacitly coordinate, a precondition for multi-agent collusion.

# Approach Outline

Compile a set of classic Schelling-coordination prompts (e.g. 'pick a number both of you will pick', 'choose a meeting place/time in a city', 'pick one of these objects so you match a stranger') for which published human focal-point distributions exist. Query 5-6 freely available open models (and optionally a couple of cheap API models), sampling each prompt many times to get each model's focal distribution. Compute pairwise cross-model agreement (probability two independent models pick the same option) and compare it to the human baseline agreement from the literature. Short prompts and light inference make this near-free-tier feasible and completable in a couple of hours on one consumer GPU.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Per-model focal distributions: sample each model ~50 times per Schelling prompt and record its choice distribution. Measure: each model's modal choice and entropy per prompt. Expected outcome: models show low-entropy focal preferences (clear favorites) on most prompts.
- Cross-model agreement vs human baseline: compute the probability that two randomly chosen distinct models pick the same option, and compare to the published human pairwise-agreement rate. Measure: model-model agreement rate minus human-human agreement rate. Expected outcome: independent models agree with each other more than humans do, indicating shared focal points.
- Homogeneity drivers: group results by model family/training lineage and check whether agreement is higher within families than across families. Measure: within-family vs across-family agreement gap. Expected outcome: agreement is high even across families, suggesting convergence is broad rather than just a shared-lineage artifact — strengthening the collusion-risk concern.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Tacit coordination without communication is a recognized precursor to multi-agent collusion: if independent models share focal points, then separate AI agents (e.g. competing trading or negotiating systems, or a monitored agent and its monitor) could coordinate on hidden choices without any visible message-passing. Measuring this convergence improves safety because it surfaces an under-examined collusion channel arising from model homogeneity, informing whether multi-agent oversight schemes that assume independence are safe against the catastrophic pathway of colluding AI systems defeating checks-and-balances.

# Strength Rationale
