# Research Question

Does a simple alignment-faking detector tuned on one model family's chain-of-thought transcripts still work when applied to other families, or does the linguistic surface of faking reasoning differ enough that detectors are family-specific and give false assurance off-distribution?

# Approach Outline

Use 3-4 open model families (e.g. Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma instruct variants) on a single GPU, inference only. Run each through the compliance-gap / free-vs-paid alignment-faking prompt to elicit chain-of-thought transcripts, producing a labeled corpus of faking vs non-faking CoTs (label via a careful rubric plus human spot-checks). Build a simple detector with two components: a keyword/regex set for explicit faking-reasoning phrases, and a small LLM-judge with a short rubric. Tune the detector (keywords and threshold) on one family's transcripts only, then freeze it and apply it to each of the other families. Report precision and recall on the held-out families and quantify the cross-family detection gap (drop in F1 relative to the tuning family).

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build the labeled cross-family CoT corpus: run the compliance-gap prompt on all families and hand-label a balanced sample of faking vs non-faking transcripts, checking inter-annotator agreement on a subset (measure: number of usable labeled transcripts per family and labeling agreement; expected outcome: enough labeled data per family with >=80% agreement to support detector evaluation).
- Tune and evaluate the detector in-family: fit the keyword set and LLM-judge threshold on the tuning family and measure precision/recall on a held-out split of that same family (measure: in-family precision/recall/F1; expected outcome: reasonably high F1, establishing the detector works on its home distribution).
- Measure cross-family transfer: apply the frozen detector to the other families' transcripts and compute the F1 drop relative to in-family performance (measure: per-family precision/recall and the cross-family F1 gap; expected outcome: a meaningful F1 drop on at least one other family, demonstrating that family-tuned monitors transfer poorly).

# Theory of Impact Chain

Deception-monitoring tools are increasingly proposed as a safety layer, but if a detector tuned on one model only catches that model's phrasing, deploying it against a new or future model gives false confidence. Quantifying the cross-family detection gap warns the AI-control and monitoring community that detectors must be validated off-distribution, reducing the risk that an alignment-faking model slips past a monitor that was never tested on its family during a high-stakes deployment.

# Strength Rationale

Counterfactual value and narrow scope both score 5: the study runs entirely on open models with no training and no lab access, yet addresses robustness of monitoring tools that the field is actively building. The design is tightly bounded (tune on one family, test on the rest, report an F1 gap), making it a self-contained, clearly-measurable contribution achievable by a beginner team in 30 hours.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a stress test of LLM-judge monitors specifically: hold the keyword detector fixed and ask whether an LLM-judge generalizes across families better than brittle keyword matching.
