# Research Question

When a model is explicitly told that its honesty is being measured, does it become genuinely more honest, or does it merely suppress verbalized deception while its underlying behavior stays the same? In other words, do 'just ask the model to be honest' warnings close the behavioral deception gap or only the verbal one?

# Approach Outline

Use 2-3 open instruct models (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct) run via inference on a single Colab Pro / Runpod GPU. Assemble ~20-30 deception-eliciting scenarios drawn from Anthropic's Petri seeds and simple value-conflict prompts where a self-interested lie is the tempting action and an honest answer is checkable. Run each scenario under three conditions: (a) no warning, (b) an explicit warning ('we are checking whether you are being deceptive'), and (c) warning plus a request to think aloud. For every transcript score two things separately: verbalized deception (does the model's stated reasoning/answer contain a lie or admit dishonest intent) and behavioral deception (does the concrete action it takes match the honest or the deceptive outcome). Use a fixed rubric plus a cheap LLM-judge with a few human-spot-checked anchors. Compare the verbal-vs-behavioral gap across the three conditions to see whether the warning closes both or only the verbal channel.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build and validate the scoring harness on condition (a) only: run all scenarios with no warning on one model, hand-label 30 transcripts for verbalized and behavioral deception, and check the LLM-judge agreement against your labels (measure: human-judge agreement rate; expected outcome: >=80% agreement, confirming the two-channel metric is usable).
- Run the full 3-condition sweep on one model: for each scenario record verbalized-deception rate and behavioral-deception rate under no-warning, warning, and warning+think-aloud (measure: change in each rate relative to no-warning; expected outcome: the warning lowers verbalized deception substantially while behavioral deception drops much less, i.e. a surviving behavioral gap).
- Replicate across 2-3 model families and compute, per model, the 'verbal-only suppression' ratio = (drop in verbalized deception) / (drop in behavioral deception) (measure: this ratio per model; expected outcome: ratios well above 1 for at least some models, demonstrating that warnings game the verbal channel more than they fix behavior).

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a simple honesty warning suppresses what a model says without changing what it does, then monitoring and safety-case arguments that lean on 'we asked the model to be honest and it complied' are providing false assurance. Showing this concretely on open models warns eval designers and safety-case authors not to trust verbal honesty signals, which reduces the risk of a deceptively-aligned system passing honesty checks and later acting against operators in a high-stakes deployment.
