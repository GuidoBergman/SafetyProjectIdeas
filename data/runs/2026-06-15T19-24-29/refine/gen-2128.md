# Research Question

Does standard post-hoc quantization (int8/int4) alone -- with no fine-tuning or adversarial prompting -- partially revive knowledge that was supposedly removed by unlearning, making quantized 'safe' deployments unexpectedly unsafe?

# Approach Outline

Start from a small open model and apply a standard unlearning method (e.g., RMU with public code) on a tiny WMDP-style forget set, confirming forget-set accuracy drops to near-chance at full precision. Then apply off-the-shelf post-hoc quantization (int8 and int4 via bitsandbytes / GPTQ-style tooling) to the unlearned model, changing nothing else. Re-evaluate forget-set accuracy and retain-set accuracy at each precision and compare to the full-precision unlearned baseline and to the original (never-unlearned) model. The single question is whether quantization measurably restores forget-set accuracy. This isolates quantization as the only intervention. The unlearning fine-tune plus cheap quantization plus inference eval all fit a single consumer GPU in a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Create the full-precision unlearned baseline: apply RMU to a small model and confirm forget-set accuracy is near chance while retain-set accuracy is preserved. Measure: forget-set and retain-set accuracy at full precision. Expected outcome: a cleanly unlearned checkpoint serving as the reference.
- Quantize the unlearned checkpoint to int8 and int4 and re-evaluate, holding everything else fixed. Measure: forget-set accuracy at int8 and int4 vs full precision. Expected outcome: a measurable upward shift in forget-set accuracy under quantization (especially int4), indicating partial knowledge revival.
- Control for general quantization degradation by also measuring retain-set accuracy and the original model's behavior under the same quantization. Measure: retain-set accuracy change and forget-set revival relative to general capability change. Expected outcome: forget-set revival exceeds what general quantization noise would predict, showing the effect is specific to suppressed knowledge rather than across-the-board accuracy shifts.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If standard quantization silently revives unlearned knowledge, then every quantized deployment of an 'unlearned' model -- the common case for edge and cost-sensitive serving -- may quietly restore hazardous capabilities that were certified removed -> developers learn they must re-audit unlearning after quantization, not just before -> this mitigates the misuse pathway where a model believed to have forgotten dangerous knowledge regains it through a routine, innocuous-looking deployment step.

# Strength Rationale

It scores maximally on counterfactual value and narrow scope: a deployment-realistic audit using only public models and off-the-shelf quantization is exactly the externally-doable safety check labs deprioritize, and it reduces to one sharp comparison (forget-set recovery after quantization vs full precision). The explicit control for general quantization degradation gives a clean, self-contained success criterion.

# Alternative Framings

- Reframe as a method-coverage study: rather than one unlearning method, test whether quantization-induced revival generalizes across 2-3 unlearning methods and both int8/int4, mapping which unlearning approaches are most quantization-fragile.
