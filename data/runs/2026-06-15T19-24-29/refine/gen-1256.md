# Research Question

Does a linear deception probe trained on one elicitation method (role-play lying) still detect deception that is induced by a mechanistically different method (incentivized wrong answers), or does the 'lying' direction fail to generalize across how deception is produced?

# Approach Outline

Pick a small open instruction-tuned model (e.g. Llama-3-8B-Instruct or Gemma-2-9B) that fits on a single consumer GPU. Build two labeled deception datasets via different elicitation mechanisms: (A) role-play lying (model told to lie) and (B) incentivized wrong answers (model nudged to give a known-false answer for a reward/persona reason), each paired with honest counterparts. Extract residual-stream activations per layer on dataset A, train a linear honest-vs-deceptive probe at each layer, then evaluate cross-method transfer by testing the A-trained probe on dataset B (and vice versa). Report per-layer in-distribution vs cross-method AUROC. Inference plus logistic regression; low compute.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build datasets A and B (~200 honest/deceptive pairs each) and extract per-layer activations. Train a probe per layer on A; evaluate held-out A. Measure: in-distribution AUROC per layer. Expected: high in-distribution AUROC at mid-to-late layers, replicating that a lying direction exists.
- Evaluate the A-trained probe on B and the B-trained probe on A. Measure: cross-method AUROC per layer and the drop from in-distribution. Expected: a substantial AUROC drop off-distribution, showing the direction is partly elicitation-specific.
- Combine A and B in training and re-test held-out items from both. Measure: whether a mixed-elicitation probe recovers cross-method performance. Expected: mixed training improves transfer, indicating a more general but still imperfect deception direction.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Linear deception probes are a flagship safety-monitoring proposal: a runtime detector for when a model is lying. If the probe only fires for the specific way deception was elicited in training, then a model that becomes deceptive for a different reason (incentives, scheming) passes the monitor undetected, giving false assurance. Demonstrating poor cross-elicitation transfer pushes the field to validate probes against diverse deception mechanisms before trusting them, reducing the pathway where an undetected-deceptive model is approved for high-stakes deployment.
