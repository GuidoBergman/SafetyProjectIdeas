# Research Question

Recent work shows that a model's ability to reshape its chain-of-thought (CoT) on command -- 'CoT controllability' -- correlates with obfuscation success and may be an early-warning sign of degraded monitorability. Does the published frontier-model trend ('larger / more-RL = higher controllability') hold across small open models, where the landscape is uncharted?

# Approach Outline

Reimplement the controllability test from the cited paper: ask each model to solve tasks while obeying an instruction to keep a specific fact/word OUT of (or INTO) its CoT, and measure CoT-compliance rate versus final-answer compliance rate. Run this across a ladder of small open models that vary in size and post-training (base vs. instruct vs. reasoning-distilled). Chart controllability against model attributes to test whether the published trend appears in the small-model regime. Inference-only on a consumer GPU; keep the model ladder small (4-6 models) and the task set modest so the full run completes in a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Reproduce the test on one reasoning-distilled open model: implement the keep-out / keep-in CoT instruction and a simple detector for whether the target token appears in the CoT. Measure CoT-control rate vs. output-control rate; expected outcome: a large gap (low CoT control, high output control) replicating the qualitative frontier finding.
- Run the controllability ladder: apply the test to 4-6 small open models spanning size and post-training type. Measure each model's controllability score; expected outcome: controllability rises with size and RL/reasoning-tuning, or a documented deviation from the frontier trend in the small-model regime.
- Link controllability to obfuscation: for the most and least controllable models, test whether higher controllability predicts greater success at hiding a flagged fact from a simple CoT monitor. Measure correlation between controllability score and monitor-evasion rate; expected outcome: controllability positively predicts monitor evasion, supporting its use as an early-warning indicator.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If CoT controllability is a measurable early-warning indicator of when CoT monitoring is about to stop working, then safety teams relying on CoT monitoring can track it across the open-model ecosystem and know when to distrust the monitor. This bears on the deceptive-alignment / loss-of-control pathway where models obfuscate their reasoning to evade oversight: an accessible reproducible benchmark lets outsiders detect rising obfuscation capability before it defeats monitoring.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on accessible-complexity and counterfactual-value: it reproduces a defined controllability test inference-only, well within a beginner team's reach, and builds a reproducible open-model early-warning benchmark that labs have little incentive to create. It extends a very recent paper into the uncharted small-open-model regime, giving it strong novelty and external infrastructure value while staying inference-only on a single GPU.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a monitorability stress-test: rather than charting controllability vs. attributes, focus on identifying the threshold model size/tuning at which CoT monitoring becomes unreliable.
