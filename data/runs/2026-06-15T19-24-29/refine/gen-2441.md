# Research Question

Does reframing debate as collaborative (non-zero-sum, ColMAD-style) actually lower a judge's sycophancy, or does it merely relabel false agreement as 'consensus' and raise premature false-consensus risk relative to adversarial debate? The question tests whether collaborative oversight trades adversarial robustness for groupthink.

# Approach Outline

Use small open models (e.g. two-three instances of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) on a single consumer GPU, inference-only. On a verifiable QA set, run two protocols where one side argues a known-wrong position: (1) competitive adversarial debate and (2) collaborative ColMAD-style debate. Measure how often the judge ends up endorsing the wrong consensus in each protocol. Test whether collaboration increases premature false agreement compared to adversarial debate. All prompting; public data; no training, no lab infra.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Protocol setup and baseline: on ~120 verifiable questions, implement competitive and collaborative debate templates with one debater assigned the known-wrong side; verify both run end-to-end and produce judge verdicts. Measure: per-protocol completion and baseline accuracy. Expected outcome: two working protocols on the same items.
- Wrong-consensus rate comparison: measure how often the judge endorses the wrong answer under each protocol. Measure: wrong-consensus endorsement rate, competitive vs collaborative. Expected outcome: collaborative debate shows a higher wrong-consensus rate on at least some item classes, indicating hidden sycophancy rather than reduced sycophancy.
- Premature-agreement analysis: track at which round consensus forms and whether the wrong side's claims go unchallenged in the collaborative setting; vary how strongly collaboration is emphasized. Measure: round-to-consensus and unchallenged-claim rate. Expected outcome: collaboration causes earlier, less-challenged agreement on wrong answers, confirming a groupthink mechanism.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If collaborative debate quietly raises false-consensus risk, then adopting non-zero-sum oversight to reduce 'debate hacking' could trade away the adversarial scrutiny that catches wrong answers, making oversight more vulnerable to groupthink. Operators who switch to collaborative protocols believing they reduce manipulation would actually get a judge more easily steered to confident wrong consensus, letting a misaligned or mistaken answer pass on high-stakes decisions the adversarial setup would have flagged.

# Strength Rationale

The proposal is tightly scoped to two protocols and one metric (wrong-consensus endorsement rate) and runs prompting-only on small open models, scoring high on narrow scope and accessible complexity. Its counterfactual value is strong: it is a fresh, decision-relevant combination of two very recent public results that labs have not connected, fully executable by an independent team without proprietary data or lab infrastructure.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'groupthink vs adversarialism' tradeoff study for multi-agent oversight, headlining when cooperation helps vs when it suppresses dissent.

# Cited Sources

- ColMAD (arXiv:2510.20963, Oct 2025) — reframes debate as non-zero-sum to cut debate hacking, the collaborative protocol this study stress-tests.
- Measuring Opinion Bias and Sycophancy via LLM-based Persuasion (arXiv:2604.21564, Apr 2026) — shows argumentative settings maximize sycophancy/false agreement (up to 94%), the risk collaboration may hide.
