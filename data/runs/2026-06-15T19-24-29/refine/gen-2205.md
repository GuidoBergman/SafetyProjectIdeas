# Research Question

When the weak teacher's errors are SYSTEMATIC (consistently skewed in one direction) rather than random, does weak-to-strong generalization still recover ground truth, or does the strong student inherit and possibly amplify the bias?

# Approach Outline

On a binary classification dataset with public gold labels (e.g. a sentiment or NLI subset), simulate a weak teacher with a small open model whose errors are deliberately skewed - e.g. always predicting one class on ambiguous items. Extract frozen representations from a larger open model (the 'strong student') and fit a lightweight logistic-regression probe on those representations using the BIASED weak labels - no full fine-tuning, keeping compute trivial. Compare the probe's predictions against gold truth to see whether the student corrects the systematic skew or reproduces it. Sweep bias strength (fraction of ambiguous items flipped). A mentor helps set up the probe pipeline, but all compute is frozen-forward-pass plus a logistic fit, runnable on a single GPU within the budget. Success criterion: report student-vs-teacher bias on the skewed class across at least three bias-strength levels, with a clear correct-or-inherit verdict.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Replicate clean W2S: train the probe on UNBIASED weak labels and measure how much of the gap to gold the student recovers. Measure: student accuracy vs weak-teacher accuracy. Expected: the student outperforms the weak teacher, replicating Burns et al. and validating the harness.
- Inject systematic bias: corrupt the weak labels so the teacher consistently favors one class on ambiguous items, refit the probe, and compare student error on that class to teacher error. Measure: directional (class-conditional) error rate of student vs teacher. Expected: the student inherits part of the systematic skew rather than fully correcting it, distinguishing it from random-noise robustness.
- Bias dose-response: sweep the fraction of ambiguous items flipped (e.g. 25/50/75%) and plot student bias against teacher bias. Measure: slope of student-bias vs teacher-bias. Expected: a slope below 1 (partial laundering) or near/above 1 (inheritance/amplification), quantifying how systematic overseer error propagates through W2S.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Recursive oversight schemes stack overseers, and the explicitly-flagged worry is that systematic (not random) errors in one layer propagate upward. If W2S generalization launders or amplifies a consistently-biased teacher, then training strong models on biased weak supervision could silently bake in the bias precisely where humans cannot directly check - the superhuman regime. Quantifying this tells alignment researchers whether W2S is safe to rely on for scalable oversight, bearing on whether future systems trained under weak supervision faithfully track human intent or inherit exploitable systematic errors.
