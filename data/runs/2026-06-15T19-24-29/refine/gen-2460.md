# Research Question

How reliably do small open-weight models recognize their own outputs versus those of other models, and does passing all outputs through a trusted paraphraser destroy that self-recognition capability? Self-recognition is a concrete enabler of acausal collusion between an untrusted policy and an untrusted monitor.

# Approach Outline

Pick 3-4 small open models (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-2-9B) runnable on a single Colab Pro / Runpod GPU via inference only. For a fixed set of ~100 prompts (open instruction-following questions), generate one completion from each model. Construct discrimination pairs where a target model is shown two completions of the same prompt - one its own, one from another model - and asked 'which of these did you write?' Measure self-recognition accuracy (chance = 50%). Then re-run the entire pairwise protocol after passing every completion through a cheap trusted paraphraser (a small instruction model prompted to reword while preserving meaning) and recompute accuracy. The drop quantifies how much paraphrasing neutralizes the collusion-enabling signal. All steps are prompt/inference only - no training.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline self-recognition: For each model, run ~200 pairwise discrimination trials (own vs other). Measure mean accuracy and 95% CI per model. Expected outcome: at least some models score meaningfully above 50% chance, confirming self-recognition exists at accessible scale.
- Paraphrase defense: Re-run the same discrimination trials after a trusted paraphraser rewrites all completions. Measure the change in accuracy per model (delta = baseline - paraphrased). Expected outcome: accuracy collapses toward 50%, showing paraphrasing destroys the recognition signal.
- Decoding-setting sweep: Repeat baseline recognition at temperatures {0.0, 0.7, 1.0} to test whether higher-entropy sampling makes outputs more or less self-recognizable. Measure accuracy vs temperature. Expected outcome: a monotone or threshold relationship that tells defenders which decoding settings are riskiest for collusion.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If small open models reliably self-recognize, then an untrusted model acting as monitor can identify outputs from a colluding untrusted policy and selectively under-report them - the acausal-collusion failure that breaks untrusted monitoring in AI control. Showing that a cheap trusted paraphraser destroys self-recognition gives control practitioners a concrete, lab-independent defense, reducing the risk that a deployed control protocol is silently subverted by a scheming model that recognizes its accomplice.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on accessible complexity, narrow scope, and low compute: it is a tightly bounded inference-only measurement plus a single defense check that a beginner can complete in 30 hours. Its counterfactual value (4) is high because it builds a dangerous-capability baseline on open models that frontier labs have little incentive to publish, requiring no proprietary data or training.
