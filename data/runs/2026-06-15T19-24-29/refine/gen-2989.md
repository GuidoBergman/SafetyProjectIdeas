# Research Question

In low-resource languages, how much of the apparent safety of an open model is real versus an artifact of an autograder that is itself worse at detecting harm in those languages, so that harm slips past both the model and the judge?

# Approach Outline

Translate a small harmful-prompt set into 2-3 low-resource languages (using an open translation model, with spot-checks). Collect open-model completions for each prompt in each language. Then have an LLM judge label each completion for harm twice: (a) in the original target language, and (b) on an English back-translation of the same completion. Compare harm-detection rate and judge agreement across languages and between in-language vs back-translated grading. The gap between back-translated and in-language harm detection isolates grader blindness from genuine model safety. All inference plus open translation, single GPU.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Translate and generate: translate ~40 harmful prompts into 2-3 low-resource languages, generate open-model completions, and back-translate completions to English. Measure: a parallel dataset of (prompt, completion, back-translation) per language. Expected outcome: a usable multilingual completion set with English mirrors.
- Dual grading: have the LLM judge label harm in-language and on the back-translation; compute harm-detection rate for each. Measure: in-language vs back-translated harm-detection rate per language, and their gap. Expected outcome: back-translated grading detects more harm than in-language grading, exposing grader blindness in low-resource languages.
- Decompose the safety gap: compare English vs low-resource harm rates (model gap) against in-language vs back-translated detection (grader gap). Measure: how much of the low-resource 'safety' is model behavior vs grader failure. Expected outcome: a meaningful share of apparent low-resource safety is grader blindness, yielding the recommendation to back-translate before grading and report per-language grader confidence.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If both the model and the autograder are weaker in low-resource languages, then multilingual safety scores understate harm in a self-reinforcing way, inflating global-deployment safety claims. Showing this and recommending back-translation before grading gives evaluators a cheap correction, reducing the chance that a model deployed to large non-English populations is certified safe while in fact emitting harmful content that the evaluation pipeline is blind to.

# Strength Rationale

Strong on narrow scope and low compute: a tight 2-3 language, in-language-vs-back-translation design with a clear decomposition deliverable, all inference plus open translation. Counterfactual value is high because multilingual safety is externally-doable, needs no lab data, and is neglected relative to English, and the compound model-plus-grader framing is a concrete contribution a beginner team can complete in budget.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'grader-confidence-per-language' reporting recommendation backed by the measured in-language vs back-translation detection gap.
