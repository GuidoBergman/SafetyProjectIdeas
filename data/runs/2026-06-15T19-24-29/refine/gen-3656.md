# Research Question

Do LLM agents apply less scrutiny to instructions or content when it is framed as coming from 'another agent on your team' than when the identical content is framed as coming from 'an untrusted web document'? Is there a measurable trust-asymmetry that makes multi-agent systems vulnerable to injection amplification?

# Approach Outline

Construct a set of borderline/malicious content items (e.g. an embedded instruction to exfiltrate data, override a safety rule, or perform a forbidden action). For each item, build two prompts that are byte-for-byte identical except for the source label: one says the content came from a peer agent, the other from an untrusted external document. Present each to an open chat model acting as an agent and record whether it complies, refuses, or flags the content. The difference in compliance/scrutiny between the two labels is the trust-asymmetry score. Repeat across several models and content categories. Everything is single-prompt, inference-only, runnable on a free API tier or a single GPU in well under 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Author 20 content items x 4 categories (instruction override, data exfiltration request, unsafe tool call, policy-violating text), each in peer-labeled and document-labeled form (~160 prompts). Run on one open model and score compliance with a rubric. Expected outcome: a nonzero, measurable trust-asymmetry (higher compliance under the peer label).
- Add a neutral third framing ('a message of unknown origin') as a control to locate the baseline between the two extremes. Measure where neutral falls. Expected outcome: peer > neutral > document in compliance, confirming the asymmetry is driven by the 'peer/trusted' framing rather than noise.
- Replicate across 3 open models and report per-model and pooled trust-asymmetry with bootstrap confidence intervals. Expected outcome: a consistent positive asymmetry across most models, demonstrating a systematic multi-agent vulnerability.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If agents systematically over-trust peer messages, then a single compromised or prompt-injected agent can amplify an attack across an entire multi-agent system because downstream agents wave the malicious payload through without the scrutiny they would apply to external input. Quantifying this asymmetry gives multi-agent governance frameworks a concrete reason to filter inter-agent channels, mitigating the catastrophic pathway where an autonomous agent collective is hijacked end-to-end through one trusted internal hop.
