# Research Question

If a safety-relevant steering vector (a refusal/deception direction) is built from English data only, does its behavioral effect transfer when prompts are issued in other languages on a multilingual model? If safety vectors are English-bound, a deployed steering-based safety tool can be silently bypassed by switching language.

# Approach Outline

On a small open multilingual instruction model (e.g. Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct or a multilingual Llama variant), build a refusal/deception steering vector using contrastive activation addition (CAA) from English contrastive prompt pairs only. Apply the fixed vector at inference and measure its behavioral effect (change in refusal rate on harmful prompts, or change in a deception-proxy metric) first in English, then on the same prompts translated into 3 other languages via a public translation API. Compare the steering effect size across languages to quantify transfer. Inference-only on a single GPU; the only fitting step is the cheap English CAA vector from cached activations.

# Proposed First Experiments

- English effect baseline: apply the English-built vector to English prompts and measure the change in refusal rate vs unsteered. Expected: a clear steering effect, validating the vector works in its source language.
- Cross-language transfer test: apply the identical vector to the same prompts translated into 3 other languages and measure the steering effect in each. Expected: partial or full attenuation of the effect in other languages, quantifying how English-bound the safety vector is.
- Translation-confound control: verify with a back-translation/quality check that translated prompts are faithful and that any attenuation reflects representational non-transfer, not broken prompts. Also test the unsteered refusal baseline per language to normalize. Expected: confirmation that reduced steering effect is a genuine transfer failure, not a translation artifact.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If English-built safety steering vectors do not transfer across languages, then a steering-based safety control deployed by a provider can be silently evaded simply by prompting in another language. Measuring transfer on an open multilingual model -> reveals whether steering defenses must be built per-language or in a language-agnostic way -> prevents a false sense of safety in multilingual deployments, mitigating the catastrophic pathway where harmful assistance is obtained by switching languages to escape a monitor/steer.

# Strength Rationale

It scores 5 on narrow_scope, accessible_complexity, and low_compute: a single, crisply-defined experiment (refusal-vector transfer to 3 languages) using standard CAA, public translation, and inference-only evaluation, feasible for a beginner in 30 hours. Counterfactual value (4) is strong because this under-watched cross-language safety question runs entirely on public multilingual weights with no proprietary data or lab infra, exactly the externally-doable angle labs deprioritize.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a mechanistic test of whether the model has a shared multilingual concept subspace: check whether building the vector from a mix of languages restores transfer, isolating whether the failure is representational or data-driven.
