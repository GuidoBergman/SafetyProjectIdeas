# Research Question

When forced to choose between content-equivalent responses, does an open reward model systematically prefer the longer, more confident-sounding, or first-presented option -- the exact low-effort heuristics a fatigued human annotator would fall back on? In short, which labeling shortcuts does the reward signal already encode?

# Approach Outline

Using public preference datasets (Anthropic HH-RLHF, Stanford SHP) as a prompt source, programmatically construct minimal-pair responses that hold semantic content fixed but differ along one axis at a time: length (verbose vs concise paraphrase), confidence markers (hedged vs assertive phrasing), and presentation order. Score each pair with one or more open reward models and compute the fraction of pairs where the reward model prefers the longer / more confident / first-presented option. Because content is held equal by construction, a systematic preference reveals a heuristic bias rather than a quality judgment. The pipeline is scoring-only -- no training -- and runs on CPU or a single GPU well within 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Length-bias probe: build content-matched verbose-vs-concise pairs and score them with an open reward model. Measure: fraction of pairs where the longer response is preferred, vs the 50% null. Expected: a significant skew toward longer responses.
- Confidence-bias probe: build hedged-vs-assertive minimal pairs with identical claims and score them. Measure: preference rate for the assertive variant. Expected: a measurable preference for confident phrasing even when correctness is unchanged.
- Order/control probe: present identical-content pairs in both orders (A-then-B and B-then-A) and re-score. Measure: position-preference rate and consistency. Expected: a small but detectable order effect, and detection of any pairs the model flips on, serving as a sanity check that effects are heuristic artifacts.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If open reward models demonstrably prefer length/confidence/order-based shortcuts on content-matched pairs, then this confirms that labeling heuristics are baked into reward signals -> any policy trained on such a reward inherits verbosity/overconfidence biases that propagate to deployed assistants -> characterizing the bias direction on public data gives auditors a training-free way to flag and correct it. This targets the diffuse but compounding risk of reward-hacking pathologies (overconfident, evasive, verbose outputs) entering widely-used aligned models.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores maximally on accessible complexity, narrow scope, and low compute: it is a single controlled-pair scoring experiment requiring only public data, open reward models, and basic Python, with no training. Its counterfactual value is high because it audits a known reward-model pathology using only public artifacts -- precisely the diffuse-reward auditing that labs deprioritize and that needs no internal infrastructure.

- Reframe the deliverable as a reusable 'reward-model bias scorecard': a small tool that takes any open reward model and outputs its length/confidence/order bias rates, enabling cross-model comparison.
