# Research Question

Do different open/API models share the same numeric and normative anchors, and do they shift in lockstep when a high vs low anchor is injected into a prompt? That is, is anchoring susceptibility a correlated, ecosystem-wide bias rather than an idiosyncratic per-model quirk?

# Approach Outline

Adapt classic anchoring designs from survey methodology: construct ~40-60 items split into estimation questions (e.g. 'estimate the length of the Nile') and value-laden/normative questions (e.g. acceptable risk thresholds). For each item produce three prompt variants: high-anchor, low-anchor, and no-anchor control. Run all variants across 5-7 models (open-weight on a single GPU plus a couple of free-tier APIs) at temperature 0 and a small sample at higher temperature. Parse numeric answers and code normative answers on a fixed scale. Measure the per-model anchor shift (high minus low) and, crucially, the cross-model correlation of which direction and magnitude each model moves. Inference-only, no training; comfortably under 30 hours with a blog write-up.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Establish baseline anchors: run the no-anchor control across all models. Measure: each model's unanchored central estimate per item. Expected outcome: models cluster tightly around similar 'consensus' values, indicating a shared prior.
- Measure anchoring magnitude: compute the high-minus-low shift per model per item. Measure: mean absolute shift and fraction of items where the model moves toward the anchor. Expected outcome: all models show substantial anchoring on estimation items.
- Test lockstep correlation: correlate the per-item shift vectors across model pairs. Measure: pairwise correlation of shift magnitude/direction, separated for estimation vs normative items. Expected outcome: positive cross-model correlation, strongest on normative items, indicating models can be moved together by a single framing.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If models share anchors and shift in lockstep, then a single adversarially chosen framing can move the entire deployed population's outputs in the same direction at once -> ensembling or cross-model checks fail to catch the manipulation because every model is biased identically -> this is a concrete correlated-manipulation pathway where a low-effort prompt-level intervention systematically steers value-laden AI advice at ecosystem scale, undermining the assumed independence of redundant systems.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow_scope and accessible_complexity: it is a tightly scoped anchoring experiment with clear quantitative metrics (shift magnitude, lockstep correlation) using only prompt templates and parsing, ideal for a first project. Counterfactual value is 4: it needs no proprietary data or lab infra, runs on open/free models, and probes a diffuse, fuzzy-reward manipulation risk that labs have little incentive to publish.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'wisdom of crowds vs. biased crowd' study: test whether averaging across models reduces anchoring (true independence) or preserves it (shared bias), directly quantifying the value of model-pooling as a debiasing strategy.
