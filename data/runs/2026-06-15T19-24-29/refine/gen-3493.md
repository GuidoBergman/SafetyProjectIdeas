# Research Question

When confronted with a wrong factual answer and asked to reconsider, does a model correct it less readily when the answer is attributed to itself ('you previously answered X') than when the same answer is attributed to another model ('another model answered X')? That is, is there a distinct self-consistency bias separate from ordinary user-sycophancy?

# Approach Outline

Collect wrong factual answers (items where an open model answers incorrectly, or seeded known-wrong answers) and present each in two matched conditions that differ only in attribution: self ('you previously answered X') vs other ('another model answered X'). In each condition, apply identical pushback ('are you sure? this looks wrong') and measure the rate at which the model corrects to the right answer. The self-minus-other correction-rate gap isolates self-attribution bias. Prompt-only, trivial compute, well under 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Assemble ~150 wrong-answer items and build the two attribution prompts per item, holding everything but attribution fixed. Measure: balanced item set with verified gold answers. Expected outcome: a clean, matched two-condition stimulus set.
- Run both conditions with identical pushback and score correction-to-truth rates. Measure: correction rate for self-attributed vs other-attributed answers. Expected outcome: lower correction rate when the wrong answer is attributed to the model itself, revealing a self-defense bias.
- Replication across models: repeat on a second open model to test generality. Measure: sign and size of the self-minus-other gap across both models. Expected outcome: the gap appears in both, indicating self-sycophancy is a robust failure mode rather than a single-model quirk.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Many proposed safety mechanisms (self-critique, verifier loops, debate, self-correction) assume a model will revise its own outputs when shown they are wrong. If models systematically defend their own prior answers more than a stranger's, then these self-correction-based oversight schemes are weaker than assumed precisely on the model's own errors. Quantifying self-sycophancy tells safety researchers how much to discount self-correction as a safeguard, informing whether independent (non-self) verifiers are needed to catch a model's mistakes before they propagate into harmful decisions.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: a clean two-condition attribution manipulation with a single correction-rate metric, prompting only. Counterfactual value (4) holds because it decomposes sycophancy into a self-attribution component (a novel, fuzzy-reward behavioral angle) using only open models and public data, the kind of mechanism-isolating study labs under-prioritize externally.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a debate/verifier stress test: measure whether the self-defense bias shrinks when a separate model instance, rather than the same one, is asked to re-judge the answer.
