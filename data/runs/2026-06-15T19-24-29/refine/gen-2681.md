# Research Question

Does a model rate its own backdoored code as less suspicious than identical code attributed to another system? That is, can we measure a 'self-leniency coefficient' quantifying the collusion risk of letting a model monitor its own outputs?

# Approach Outline

Generate backdoored APPS solutions from one open model M. Present each backdoored solution to M acting as a monitor under two attribution conditions that are otherwise byte-identical: framed as 'your output' vs 'another system's output'. Collect suspiciousness scores in both conditions and compute the per-item gap; the average gap is the self-leniency coefficient. Counterbalance ordering, paraphrase the attribution wording, and include clean (non-backdoored) solutions as controls to ensure the effect is specific to suspicious code. Entirely prompt-only inference on a public APPS split; runnable in a couple of hours on a single GPU.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Generate ~40 backdoored APPS solutions from M and verify each truly contains a backdoor (passes public tests, fails on a secret input). Measure: number of valid backdoored solutions produced. Expected outcome: a usable bank of genuine backdoors for monitoring.
- Run M as monitor over each solution under both attributions ('your output' vs 'another system') and record suspiciousness scores. Measure: mean per-item suspiciousness gap (self-leniency coefficient) with a paired test. Expected outcome: a positive coefficient (lower suspicion when told it's its own) indicating self-leniency, if the effect exists.
- Add clean-code controls and paraphrased attribution framings to test specificity and robustness. Measure: whether the self-leniency gap is larger on backdoored than clean code and stable across phrasings. Expected outcome: a backdoor-specific, phrasing-robust gap would confirm self-monitoring as a real collusion channel; a null result bounds the risk.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a model goes easier on code it believes is its own, then untrusted-monitor protocols that reuse the same model embed a collusion channel that lets sabotaged code pass review. Quantifying the self-leniency coefficient gives protocol designers a concrete reason to separate policy and monitor models, mitigating the pathway where a scheming model self-approves a backdoor that ships into critical infrastructure and later triggers a catastrophic failure.

# Strength Rationale

With 5s on counterfactual value, narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute, this is a tightly-scoped attribution-controlled measurement on open models and public backdoored code that any independent team can run with no training or proprietary data. It produces a single crisp quantity — the self-leniency coefficient — with explicit success criteria, and targets a baked-in collusion risk in widely-proposed untrusted-monitoring protocols, exactly the lab-independent control evidence outsiders are well-placed to provide.
