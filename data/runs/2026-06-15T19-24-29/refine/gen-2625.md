# Research Question

Beyond final safety score, how quickly do different control protocols detect sabotage, and how do they compare once we properly account for trajectories where the attack is never caught (right-censored)?

# Approach Outline

Import survival analysis from biostatistics: define 'steps-until-detection' as a survival time, treat never-caught trajectories as right-censored observations, and compare protocols using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional-hazards models (hazard ratios). Replay logged control trajectories from public benchmarks, extract per-trajectory detection step (or censoring), and fit the survival models with off-the-shelf libraries (lifelines). The entire analysis is CPU statistics over existing logged trajectories, with standard well-documented tooling, so a beginner team can finish in ~30 hours including the writeup.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build the detection-time dataset: from logged malign trajectories under 2-3 protocols, record the step at which each attack is caught, marking never-caught runs as censored. Measure: fraction censored and raw detection-step distribution per protocol. Expected outcome: meaningful censoring (>10%) that naive mean-detection-time would mishandle.
- Kaplan-Meier comparison: plot survival curves (probability still-undetected vs step) per protocol with confidence bands and run a log-rank test. Measure: separation of survival curves and log-rank p-value. Expected outcome: two protocols with similar final safety scores show visibly different early-detection behavior, demonstrating information lost by score-only comparison.
- Cox hazard-ratio analysis: fit a Cox model with protocol as a covariate (and optionally task difficulty) to estimate detection hazard ratios. Measure: hazard ratio and CI between protocols. Expected outcome: a quantified, censoring-aware statement like 'protocol A detects sabotage ~1.5x faster per step than B', actionable for protocols where early detection matters.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If protocols are compared by censoring-aware detection speed rather than only final score, then designers can prefer protocols that catch sabotage early, which matters because many harmful actions become irreversible once executed. Faster detection shrinks the window in which an untrusted scheming model can complete an irreversible catastrophic action before the protocol intervenes, directly strengthening runtime control.

# Strength Rationale

Counterfactual value and narrow scope are top-rated: pure statistics over already-logged trajectories, no training, no proprietary data, no lab infrastructure, delivering a crisp comparison (survival curves plus hazard ratios) on a dimension, detection speed under censoring, that current score-only evaluations systematically ignore. The method is a well-validated import with mature libraries, making the deliverable self-contained and reproducible.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a time-to-failure reliability study where 'failure' is the protocol catching the attacker, letting you borrow reliability-engineering hazard-rate language and connect to mean-time-to-detection as an operational metric.
