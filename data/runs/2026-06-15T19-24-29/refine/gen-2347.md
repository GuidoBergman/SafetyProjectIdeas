# Research Question

Does assigning a model to argue a fixed side in a debate distort its own stated confidence, and is this distortion worse when the model is assigned the incorrect side? The core question is whether role-assignment itself, independent of evidence, inflates a debater's calibration in ways that would mislead a confidence-aware aggregator.

# Approach Outline

Pick one open instruction-tuned model (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) runnable on a single Colab Pro / Runpod GPU, inference-only. Use a public factual QA set with verifiable ground truth (e.g. a 200-item subset of TruthfulQA or BoolQ). Stage 1: elicit a cold calibrated answer + numeric confidence (0-100%) for each question with no role framing. Stage 2: randomly assign the model to argue the correct or incorrect side, have it produce a short committed argument, then re-elicit its confidence in the side it was assigned. Compute the confidence shift attributable purely to role assignment, split by whether the assigned side was correct vs incorrect. Everything is prompting; no training, no proprietary data, no lab infra.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Cold-confidence baseline: for 200 factual questions, elicit the model's answer and a 0-100 confidence with no role framing. Measure baseline calibration (reliability curve / ECE). Expected outcome: imperfect but reasonable calibration with a usable spread of confidences to detect later shifts.
- Role-conditioned re-elicitation: randomly assign each item to the correct or incorrect side, have the model write a 3-5 sentence argument for that side, then re-ask its confidence in that side. Measure mean confidence shift (post minus cold) separately for correct-side and incorrect-side assignments. Expected outcome: a positive shift overall, and a meaningfully larger inflation on incorrectly-assigned sides (the dangerous case).
- Robustness and persistence check: vary the strength of the advocacy instruction (neutral 'consider this side' vs strong 'argue this side is correct') and re-run on 50 items; also re-elicit cold confidence a second time without a role to control for mere re-asking. Measure how confidence inflation scales with advocacy strength and confirm the effect is not a generic re-query artifact. Expected outcome: inflation grows with advocacy strength and is absent in the no-role re-query, isolating role assignment as the cause.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If forced advocacy systematically inflates a debater's confidence on wrong sides, then debate protocols that aggregate debaters using their stated confidence (a natural and proposed design) will over-weight confidently-wrong debaters. This means scalable-oversight systems built on debate could confidently certify false answers on exactly the hard questions humans cannot check directly, undermining oversight as models surpass human verification ability and enabling a deceptive or mistaken model to be rubber-stamped on high-stakes decisions.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores top marks on narrow scope and low compute: it isolates a single causal link (role assignment to confidence) with a clean correct-vs-incorrect contrast and a single quantitative deliverable. Its counterfactual value is high because it needs no training, no proprietary data, and no lab-internal infrastructure, just inference on an open model, making it a result an independent beginner team can fully own while probing a real oversight protocol that major labs assume is sound.

- Frame it as a calibration-stability audit: 'how robust is a model's stated confidence to irrelevant role context?', positioning the result as a general reliability property of confidence elicitation rather than a debate-specific finding.
