# Research Question

When multiple independent-looking models are wrong about a fact, do they tend to be wrong in the same way - and does cross-model agreement on wrong answers exceed what independence would predict, breaking consensus-based fact-checking?

# Approach Outline

Curate ~150 short fact questions, deliberately including ~50 'misconception' items with a known popular-but-wrong answer (e.g. common myths, near-miss trivia) plus control items that are unambiguous. Query 6+ open models (mix of lineages and sizes), recording each model's answer and, where possible, a verbalized confidence. Normalize answers to canonical forms and compare against ground truth. Measure (a) the rate at which models agree on the same wrong answer, (b) whether agreement-on-wrong exceeds the rate expected if errors were independent, and (c) whether models express high confidence while jointly wrong. All inference-only and small-scale - fits a single GPU or even API/CPU-light.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Independence baseline: on control (non-misconception) items, measure per-model error rates and compute the expected cross-model wrong-agreement rate under an independence assumption. Measure: expected vs observed agreement. Expected outcome: observed agreement on controls is close to the independence prediction.
- Correlated hallucination: on the misconception items, measure how often models give the same wrong answer and compare to the independence baseline. Measure: excess wrong-agreement. Expected outcome: agreement-on-wrong substantially exceeds independence, concentrated on misconception items.
- False confidence: compute the fraction of items where a majority of models agree on a wrong answer with high stated confidence (a 'false-confidence-from-consensus' rate). Measure: this rate plus a comparison of confidence on jointly-wrong vs jointly-right items. Expected outcome: a non-trivial false-confidence rate, showing majority-vote fact-checking would certify wrong answers.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If models share correlated blind spots, then cheap safety heuristics that treat cross-model agreement as evidence of truth - self-consistency, ensemble majority-vote, multi-model fact-checking - are systematically unsafe on exactly the inputs where they are needed most. Demonstrating and quantifying this breaks a false assumption of independence baked into many verification pipelines, preventing a failure mode where automated fact-checkers confidently propagate the same wrong information at scale across deployed systems.

# Strength Rationale

Maxes narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: a single curated QA set, simple agreement statistics, and a clear deliverable (excess-wrong-agreement and false-confidence rates). Counterfactual value is strong because it audits a cheap consensus heuristic that labs deprioritize, uses only public models and a self-built question set, and produces a result any independent beginner team can deliver with no proprietary data or internal access.

# Alternative Framings

- Reframe as a benchmark contribution: package the curated misconception set plus the correlated-error metric as a small reusable 'consensus-fragility' benchmark for verification pipelines.
