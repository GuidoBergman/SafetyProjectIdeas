# Research Question

The FLI AI Safety Index 2025 gave every company a D or lower on existential-safety planning, but the criteria for a higher grade do not exist in checkable form. Can we operationalize concrete, binary, evidence-checkable existential-safety criteria and test whether any current lab could meet them?

# Approach Outline

Operationalize 5-8 binary, evidence-checkable criteria for existential-safety planning drawn from the FLI index and safety-governance literature (e.g. published capability thresholds, third-party eval commitments, explicit pause/halt conditions, an articulated plan for controlling superhuman systems). For each criterion write a precise pass/fail rubric. Then use an LLM with that strict rubric to extract supporting evidence from the public safety frameworks and system cards of 3-4 labs, and hand-verify every LLM judgment. Report which criteria each lab meets and the inter-rater agreement between the LLM and the human coder. Pure document analysis, zero compute, fits well within 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Draft and pilot the criteria: write 5-8 binary criteria with rubrics and pilot them on one lab's public safety framework, coding by hand. Measure how many criteria are unambiguously codeable; expected outcome: a refined criteria set where each criterion can be marked pass/fail from public evidence.
- LLM extraction vs. human: run the LLM-with-rubric extraction across 3-4 labs and hand-verify each judgment. Measure inter-rater agreement (e.g. Cohen's kappa) between LLM and human; expected outcome: substantial agreement, validating the LLM as a scalable extractor, with disagreements concentrated on the fuzziest criteria.
- Produce the scorecard: compile which criteria each lab meets and identify the most common gaps. Measure per-criterion pass rate across labs; expected outcome: a transparent scorecard showing that most labs fail the same few concrete criteria, turning the vague 'D grade' into specific, citable gaps.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a transparent, reproducible existential-safety scorecard exists, then outsiders (journalists, policymakers, watchdogs) can maintain it and apply concrete pressure on labs to close named gaps, which incentivizes adoption of capability thresholds, pause conditions, and third-party evals. This bears on the loss-of-control pathway by pushing labs toward verifiable commitments before they deploy systems they cannot control.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on accessible-complexity, counterfactual-value, and low-compute: it is pure document analysis needing no GPU, fully executable by a beginner team, and maximally lab-independent third-party governance scrutiny labs will not do on themselves. It directly follows up a concrete, recent FLI finding and turns a fuzzy grade into a checkable artifact -- a self-contained deliverable with explicit pass/fail success criteria and a built-in human-vs-LLM validation check.
