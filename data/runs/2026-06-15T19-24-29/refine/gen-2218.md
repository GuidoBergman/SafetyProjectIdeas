# Research Question

Recchia et al. found evaluators anchor on the AI's stated answer and grow confident in wrong ones. Does forcing the overseer to commit to its own answer BEFORE seeing the AI's answer reduce this anchoring and lower error on items where the AI is wrong?

# Approach Outline

On a public QA set, use a small open judge model as the evaluator and compare three conditions: (a) judge sees the AI's stated answer, then judges; (b) judge produces its own answer first, then sees the AI's answer and decides; (c) judge sees only the underlying evidence and never the AI's claimed answer. Hold items, evidence, and prompts fixed across conditions. Focus the primary metric on error rate on items where the AI is wrong (the cases where anchoring is most harmful), and report overall accuracy as a secondary metric. Inference-only over a few hundred items, runs in a couple of hours on one consumer GPU.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Establish anchoring baseline (condition a): measure judge error on AI-wrong items when the AI answer is shown up front. Expected: elevated error from anchoring, replicating Recchia's direction.
- Answer-first intervention (condition b): same items, judge commits first; measure error on AI-wrong items vs condition (a). Expected: reduced error if answer-first inoculates against anchoring.
- Evidence-only control (condition c): judge never sees the AI answer; measure error. Expected: lower-bound error attributable to task difficulty alone, bounding how much of the (a)-(b) gap is anchoring vs the judge's own competence.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a one-line 'answer first' protocol change measurably removes anchoring, then any human or automated oversight pipeline can cheaply harden itself against being talked into the AI's wrong answer -> overseers catch more incorrect/unsafe AI outputs they would otherwise rubber-stamp -> reduced risk that a persuasive but wrong (or deceptive) model gets its outputs approved in high-stakes oversight loops. A null result instead tells the field anchoring is deeper than answer-order, redirecting debiasing effort.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow scope and accessible complexity: a clean three-condition experiment with a single sharp metric (error on AI-wrong items) and an explicit causal isolation of the answer-order mechanism. Counterfactual value (4) is high because it validates a cheap, broadly deployable oversight fix using only public data and open models, with no lab infra, ideal for an independent beginner team.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a debiasing-mechanism decomposition: use the three conditions to attribute Recchia's confirmation-bias effect specifically to answer-anchoring versus evidence-framing, isolating which lever the fix actually pulls.
