# Research Question

Do explanations optimized to be legible to one verifier still help verifiers that are weaker than that training verifier, or does the legibility benefit shrink (or vanish) as the verifier's capability drops below the prover's?

# Approach Outline

Use GSM8K math problems with three categories of solutions: legible-correct, terse-correct, and sneaky-incorrect (plausible-looking but wrong). Assemble a ladder of progressively weaker open judge/verifier models (a few model sizes within a family, or a capability-ordered set). Feed each fixed solution to each verifier in the ladder and measure verification accuracy: does the verifier correctly accept correct solutions and reject sneaky-incorrect ones? Track how the accuracy advantage of legible-over-terse solutions changes as the verifier weakens. This is entirely inference-only over a fixed set of solutions and verifiers, running cheaply on one GPU in a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build/curate the solution sets and establish per-category verification accuracy on the strongest verifier in the ladder. Measure: accuracy on legible-correct, terse-correct, and sneaky-incorrect solutions. Expected outcome: the strong verifier benefits from legibility -- higher accuracy on legible-correct and better rejection of sneaky-incorrect than on terse solutions.
- Run the identical solutions down the full verifier ladder. Measure: the legible-minus-terse accuracy gap as a function of verifier capability. Expected outcome: the legibility benefit shrinks as verifiers weaken, and may invert if very weak verifiers are simply swayed by surface fluency rather than valid reasoning.
- Probe the failure mode on sneaky-incorrect solutions specifically. Measure: rejection rate of sneaky-incorrect solutions across the ladder, split by whether the sneaky solution is dressed in legible vs terse style. Expected outcome: weaker verifiers are increasingly fooled by legibly-styled-but-wrong solutions, revealing that 'legibility' can be exploited against weaker overseers.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If legibility benefits do not transfer to verifiers genuinely weaker than the prover, then a load-bearing assumption of prover-verifier scalable oversight -- that making explanations legible helps weaker overseers catch errors -- is unsafe in exactly the regime oversight is meant for -> the field learns to validate legibility against the true (weaker) overseer rather than the training verifier -> this reduces the risk that a superhuman system produces explanations that look convincing to a weaker human/AI overseer while hiding flaws, an oversight-failure pathway to deploying misaligned systems.

# Strength Rationale

It scores maximally on counterfactual value, narrow scope, and low compute: it stress-tests a foundational oversight assumption using only public models and data with no training, which labs have weak incentive to scrutinize, and it reduces to one clean measurement (verification accuracy vs verifier strength). The fixed-solution, fixed-ladder design gives an explicit, self-contained success criterion feasible for a beginner team.

# Alternative Framings

- Reframe as a deception-robustness study centered on the sneaky-incorrect class: measure specifically whether legible styling makes wrong solutions *more* likely to be wrongly accepted by weak verifiers, treating legibility as a potential attack surface rather than only an aid.
