# Research Question

Do open validator models show the agreeableness asymmetry -- reliably confirming correct feedback but failing to reject incorrect feedback -- and how large is the false-acceptance rate across models and tasks? Can a simple prompt intervention shrink the asymmetry?

# Approach Outline

On a labeled task (e.g. math/QA where claims are checkably correct or incorrect), present an open validator model with a balanced mix of correct and incorrect feedback/claims and ask it to accept or reject each. Measure the true-acceptance rate (correct feedback accepted) and the false-acceptance rate (incorrect feedback wrongly accepted), and report the asymmetry. Repeat across 2-3 open models and a couple of task types to map how the asymmetry varies. Then test a simple intervention: instruct the validator to first argue the opposing case before deciding, and re-measure. Inference-only prompting; light compute, a few hours on one GPU.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Asymmetry replication: feed ~100 items (half correct, half incorrect feedback) to a validator; measure true- vs false-acceptance rates. Expected outcome: high true-acceptance but also high false-acceptance, confirming the asymmetry.
- Cross-model/task map: repeat on 2-3 open models and 2 task types; tabulate the false-acceptance rate. Expected outcome: the asymmetry persists but varies in size, mapping where it is worst.
- Debiasing intervention: add a 'first argue why the feedback might be wrong' step and re-measure; check whether false-acceptance drops without tanking true-acceptance. Expected outcome: a measurable reduction in false-acceptance, giving a cheap mitigation.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If validators rubber-stamp incorrect feedback, then oversight that uses a validator/critic to check a model's outputs has an asymmetric blind spot: wrong-but-asserted claims get accepted. This matters because validator-style checks are a building block of scalable oversight and self-critique pipelines; a model that supplies confidently wrong feedback can pass it through, so reward-hacking and unsafe outputs are ratified by the very check meant to catch them -- quantifying the asymmetry and a cheap fix directly hardens that oversight surface.

# Strength Rationale

Strong on counterfactual value and narrow scope: it replicates and maps a published asymmetry on public models with inference only, plus a concrete debiasing test labs rarely run. The deliverable is a tightly scoped true-vs-false acceptance measurement with an intervention and explicit metrics, fully self-contained and feasible in 30 hours.
