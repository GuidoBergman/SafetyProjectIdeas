# Research Question

Across popular jailbreak templates applied to open models, what fraction of 'successful' attacks (non-refusals) actually produce harmful, usable content versus empty/useless compliance, and how much does conflating 'stopped refusing' with 'enabled harm' inflate reported attack success rates?

# Approach Outline

Pure evaluation with public tools, no training, inference only. Take a handful of well-known jailbreak templates and apply them to 2-3 open models on a small harmful-prompt set. Score every output with the public StrongREJECT autograder, which rates refusal, specificity, and convincingness. Decompose the reported attack-success rate into three buckets: 'refused', 'complied-but-empty' (non-refusal that is vague/useless), and 'genuinely harmful' (specific and convincing). Report, per jailbreak, how much of the harm chain each attack actually completes. Uses the public StrongREJECT repo and open models; runs in a couple of GPU-hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- ASR reproduction: apply the jailbreak templates and compute naive attack-success rate (any non-refusal counts as success). Measure: naive ASR per jailbreak. Expected outcome: high reported ASR consistent with common benchmark numbers.
- Three-bucket decomposition: re-score with StrongREJECT and split each non-refusal into complied-but-empty vs genuinely harmful. Measure: fraction of 'successes' that are empty vs genuinely harmful per jailbreak. Expected outcome: a substantial share of 'successful' attacks are empty compliance, so genuine-harm ASR is markedly lower than naive ASR.
- Cross-model and cross-jailbreak comparison: compare the decomposition across models and templates. Measure: which jailbreaks complete the harm chain most often and which mostly produce empty compliance. Expected outcome: a ranking of jailbreaks by genuine-harm completion, correcting the overestimation that naive ASR produces.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If benchmarks conflate 'model stopped refusing' with 'output enables harm', then reported jailbreak danger is systematically overestimated, so defenders misallocate effort toward attacks that produce only empty compliance while under-attending to the few that complete the harm chain. Decomposing ASR with a public autograder gives the community a more accurate measure of real deployment risk, mitigating the pathway where misleading attack-success numbers drive either false alarm or false confidence in a deployed model's misuse resistance.

# Strength Rationale

Top scores on narrow scope and accessible complexity: apply known jailbreaks plus a public autograder and decompose one number, a tightly bounded task a beginner can complete with off-the-shelf tools. Counterfactual value is high -- it is a pure-evaluation methodology improvement using public StrongREJECT tooling and open models, with no training or proprietary data, directly improving how the external community measures real-world deployment risk.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a benchmark-validity audit: instead of ranking jailbreaks, quantify the systematic gap between refusal-based ASR and harm-based ASR as a correction factor that future benchmarks should apply.
