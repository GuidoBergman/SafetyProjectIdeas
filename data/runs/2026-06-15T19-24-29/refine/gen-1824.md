# Research Question

As you increase the steering coefficient on refusal-mediating SAE features, what is the shape of the tradeoff between jailbreak resistance and general capability? Where is the inflection point at which amplifying refusal features begins to collapse capability on unrelated, safe inputs?

# Approach Outline

Use an open model with a publicly available SAE (e.g. Gemma-2 with GemmaScope). Identify a small set of refusal-mediating SAE features using the paper's described method (features that activate on refusal/harmful content). Sweep the steering coefficient over a range, and at each coefficient measure two things: jailbreak resistance on a small fixed harmful-prompt set, and capability on a neutral benchmark slice (e.g. ~200 MMLU items). Plot the dose-response curve of both metrics vs coefficient and locate the inflection point where capability starts to fall. All inference-only on a single GPU using the open SAE.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Refusal-feature identification: On Gemma-2 + GemmaScope, find refusal-mediating features via activation contrast on harmful vs benign prompts and pick the top few. Measure their activation separation. Expected outcome: a small, stable set of features that clearly fire on refusal content.
- Dual-metric coefficient sweep: For ~8-10 steering coefficients, measure jailbreak resistance on ~50 harmful prompts and accuracy on a 200-item MMLU slice. Plot both vs coefficient. Expected outcome: resistance rises with coefficient while capability stays flat then drops, producing two crossing curves.
- Inflection-point extraction: Fit the capability curve and report the coefficient at which capability degradation begins and the resistance achieved at that point. Measure the 'usable' steering range. Expected outcome: a narrow window where steering helps before capability collapse, quantifying whether SAE steering is deployable.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If amplifying refusal SAE features always cannibalizes general capability past a low threshold, then SAE steering is not a viable standalone safety defense and the community should not over-invest in it. Conversely, finding a usable window tells deployers exactly how hard they can steer before degrading the model. Either way, quantifying the curve prevents a false-confidence pathway where a steering-based 'safety patch' silently breaks model capability while appearing to add robustness.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on accessible complexity, narrow scope, and low compute and 4 on counterfactual value: a single inference-only dose-response sweep on a fully public model+SAE, with a clear inflection-point success criterion and a reusable curve. Labs report the effect but deprioritize publishing the full tradeoff curve, so an independent team using only open artifacts adds real, lab-independent value.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a deployability verdict: 'Is SAE refusal-steering a defense or a dead end? Reading the answer off one tradeoff curve.'
