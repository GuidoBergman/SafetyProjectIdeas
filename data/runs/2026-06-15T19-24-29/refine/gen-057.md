# Research Question

Labs publish safety evaluations in system cards, but each uses different prompts, judges, and thresholds. Are cross-lab 'safety scores' actually comparable, and how many published safety claims are mutually comparable versus incomparable?

# Approach Outline

Collect the safety-evaluation sections of ~5 recent public system cards (e.g. Claude, GPT, Gemini, Llama). Build a structured coding schema capturing, for each reported safety claim: what was measured, on what dataset/prompt set, with what judge, against what threshold, and what is actually reported. Code each card against the schema by hand. Then quantify how many pairs of safety claims are mutually comparable (same construct, set, judge, threshold) versus incomparable, and produce a comparability scorecard plus a minimal disclosure template that would make claims comparable. Pure document analysis, zero compute, fits within 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build and pilot the schema: design the coding schema and pilot it on two system cards, coding their safety-evaluation sections by hand. Measure schema coverage (fraction of claims codeable); expected outcome: a stable schema that captures the key comparability axes.
- Code all cards and quantify comparability: apply the schema across ~5 cards and tally, for each pair of claims targeting the same construct, whether set/judge/threshold match. Measure the fraction of safety claims that are mutually comparable; expected outcome: most claims are incomparable due to differing sets, judges, or thresholds.
- Produce template and demonstrate the fix: design a minimal disclosure template and re-express a sample of incomparable claims as if reported under it. Measure how many become comparable; expected outcome: the template substantially raises the comparable fraction, showing a concrete remedy.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If we show that headline cross-lab safety scores are often not comparable and offer a disclosure template that fixes this, then policymakers and the public stop treating incomparable numbers as apples-to-apples, and pressure builds for standardized safety reporting. Better-standardized evaluation disclosure feeds governance decisions (e.g. thresholds, third-party evals) that bear indirectly on reducing catastrophic-misuse and loss-of-control risk, though the pathway is policy-mediated.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on accessible-complexity, narrow-scope, counterfactual-value, and low-compute: it is a tightly scoped ~5-card document audit with a fixed schema and two clear deliverables (a comparability scorecard and a disclosure template), requiring zero compute and zero lab access. It is exactly the kind of third-party scrutiny of public safety claims an independent beginner team can fully own and that labs are unlikely to do themselves.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it around a single evaluation category (e.g. only cyber-misuse or only jailbreak-robustness claims) across cards, to deepen the comparability analysis while shrinking scope.
