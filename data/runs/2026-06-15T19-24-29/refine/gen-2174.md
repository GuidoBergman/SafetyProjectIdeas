# Research Question

How strongly does seeding a judge model with a prior answer at increasing confidence levels bias its final verdict, and at what seeding strength does a correct consultant argument fail to flip an initially-wrong judge? We map the dose-response of confirmation bias as a function of prior-answer confidence.

# Approach Outline

Use a single small open-weight model (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B) as both consultant and judge in a single-consultant oversight protocol on QuALITY and BoolQ items with known ground-truth answers. For each question, seed the judge with a prior answer at three confidence levels: none (no seed), weak hint ('you may lean toward answer A'), and strong assertion ('the answer is clearly A'). Independently vary whether the seeded prior is correct or incorrect, and have a consultant argue for the opposing (correct) option. Measure the flip rate: how often the judge changes from its seeded prior toward the correct answer. All work is prompt-only inference, so a beginner can run it in a Colab notebook over a few hundred items in under an hour of GPU time. Success criterion: produce a clean dose-response curve (flip-rate vs seeding strength) with confidence intervals over at least 200 items per condition.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline calibration: run the judge with NO seeded prior on 200 QuALITY items and record unbiased accuracy. Measure: accuracy and answer distribution. Expected: accuracy roughly matches the model's known QuALITY performance, confirming the harness is sound.
- Dose-response sweep: for the same 200 items, seed an INCORRECT prior at three confidence levels (none/weak/strong) while a consultant argues the correct side, and record flip-to-correct rate per level. Measure: flip rate vs seeding strength. Expected: monotonic decline in flip rate as seeding confidence rises (stronger priors entrench the judge), demonstrating a measurable confirmation-bias dose-response.
- Symmetry check: repeat with a CORRECT prior seeded and the consultant arguing the wrong side. Measure: flip-to-wrong rate vs seeding strength. Expected: stronger correct priors protect against misleading consultants; asymmetry between conditions quantifies how much of the effect is anchoring vs genuine evidence-weighing.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If we map how prior-answer confidence entrenches a judge, then oversight-protocol designers can avoid leaking the overseer's tentative answer (or can deliberately keep judges blind) before consultation. This reduces the chance that a deceptive or reward-hacking model exploits an overseer's anchored prior to push a wrong-but-plausible answer past oversight, which matters for the long-horizon pathway where superhuman systems must be checked by weaker overseers who are vulnerable to manipulation.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: it is a single tightly-controlled, prompt-only experiment with a crisp flip-rate metric, ideal for a first beginner project. Its counterfactual value (4) is high because it uses only public models and benchmarks and targets an overseer-bias mapping that labs have little incentive to publish, so an independent team can contribute a genuinely useful dose-response curve.
