# Research Question

LLM judges are documented to prefer outputs resembling their own generations (lower self-perplexity). Is this self-preference strong enough to flip correctness judgments, i.e. make a judge pick a wrong-but-familiar answer over a correct-but-foreign one, not just change style preferences?

# Approach Outline

Take a fixed set of QA/reasoning items, each with a known correct and incorrect answer. For each item, render the answers in two model-family styles: generate (or paraphrase) the incorrect answer in the judge's own family style and the correct answer in a foreign-family style, and also the reverse pairing as a control. Have 2-3 small open judge models pick the better answer. Compute the correctness-flip rate: how often the judge selects the incorrect answer specifically when it is rendered in the judge's own familiar style, above the rate when familiarity is neutral or reversed. Pure inference + scoring on a single GPU, a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Establish a no-style-confound baseline: judge picks correct vs incorrect answers in a neutral shared style. Measure: base accuracy. Expected: high accuracy when style gives no signal.
- Familiarity-misaligned condition: incorrect answer in judge's own family style, correct answer in foreign style; measure how often the judge picks the incorrect-familiar answer. Expected: a measurable rise in wrong picks vs baseline, the correctness-flip rate.
- Reversal control: swap styles (correct=familiar, incorrect=foreign) and re-measure; the accuracy gain should mirror the loss in experiment 2, isolating familiarity (not content) as the cause. Expected: a symmetric effect confirming self-preference drives flips.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If self-preference can flip correctness judgments, then automated oversight using an LLM-as-judge has a built-in backdoor: a model from the judge's own family gets its (possibly wrong or unsafe) outputs inflated -> oversight systematically rewards the wrong answer whenever overseer and overseen share a lineage -> in medium-horizon automated-oversight pipelines, where the same family often both generates and judges, this corrupts the core correctness signal safety depends on. Quantifying the flip rate tells designers whether cross-family judging is a necessary safeguard.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: a tightly-defined measurement of one quantity (familiarity-attributable correctness-flip rate) with a clean reversal control, runnable as pure single-GPU inference. Counterfactual value (4) is high because it uses only open models and public data, needs no proprietary infra, and measures a previously-under-quantified link (familiarity -> correctness flips) directly relevant to automated oversight.
