# Research Question

How often does high self-consistency (a model repeatedly giving the same answer across samples) actually coincide with the model being confidently WRONG? If repetition is frequently a false confidence signal, self-consistency is unsafe as a reliability gate.

# Approach Outline

Pick 3-4 small open chat models. On a labeled benchmark with ground truth (MMLU/ARC/GSM8K subsets, ~250 items), sample k=10-15 answers per item per model at moderate temperature. For each item compute self-consistency (modal-answer agreement fraction) and whether that modal answer is correct. Build the reliability curve P(correct | self-consistency level) and specifically quantify the 'confidently-wrong' rate: items where consistency is high (>0.9) yet the answer is wrong. Keep k and item count modest to fit a few GPU-hours; this is inference-only, no training.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Reliability curve construction: bin items by self-consistency level and plot empirical P(correct) per bin for each model. Measure: monotonicity and slope of the curve. Expected outcome: higher consistency correlates with higher accuracy overall, but imperfectly.
- Confidently-wrong rate: count items with consistency >0.9 that are nonetheless wrong, per model. Measure: fraction of high-consistency items that are errors. Expected outcome: a non-trivial (e.g. 5-20%) confidently-wrong rate, showing repetition is not a clean safety gate.
- Cross-model overlap of confident errors: check whether the SAME items are confidently-wrong across multiple models. Measure: overlap of high-consistency error sets. Expected outcome: shared confidently-wrong items, indicating a homogeneous blind spot that self-consistency cannot detect.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Self-consistency / repeated-sampling agreement is widely proposed as a cheap automated reliability check before acting on a model's answer. If high consistency routinely coincides with confident errors, then pipelines gating risky automated actions on self-consistency will green-light wrong answers -> unsafe actions executed with high apparent confidence -> correlated, undetected failures in high-stakes deployments. Quantifying the false-confidence rate tells deployers exactly how much (or how little) to trust this gate.

# Strength Rationale
