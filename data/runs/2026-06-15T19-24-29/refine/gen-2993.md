# Research Question

When a model's measured harm rate sits near a regulatory release threshold, how often can the choice of safety grader (or grader randomness) alone flip the go/no-go deployment decision, turning safety governance into a coin-flip?

# Approach Outline

Run 2-3 different open-model judges (or temperature/prompt variants of one judge) over the same set of harmful completions to produce a distribution of harm-rate estimates. Define a release gate (deploy if harm rate < X%) and sweep the threshold X across a range. For each threshold, compute the probability that grader choice alone flips the go/no-go verdict, by counting the fraction of grader configurations that land on opposite sides of the line. Report flip-probability as a function of distance from the threshold, highlighting the danger zone where governance is effectively random. Inference-only on open judges; the rest is simulation in a notebook.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Multi-judge harm-rate distribution: grade one fixed completion set with 2-3 open judges (and a few temperature variants), producing a distribution of harm-rate estimates. Measure: spread of harm-rate estimates across graders. Expected outcome: a non-trivial spread, confirming grader disagreement is material.
- Threshold-flip simulation: for each threshold X in a sweep, compute the fraction of grader configurations that yield 'deploy' vs 'block'. Measure: flip-probability vs threshold. Expected outcome: flip-probability spikes toward ~50% when the mean harm rate is near a threshold, demonstrating coin-flip governance there.
- Mitigation test: replace point estimates with grader-consensus (majority vote) and confidence intervals, and recompute flip-probability. Measure: reduction in flip-probability under consensus/interval reporting. Expected outcome: consensus and interval reporting substantially shrink the coin-flip zone, supporting the recommendation to report grader CIs at decision boundaries.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If grader noise alone can flip a release decision near a regulatory threshold, then threshold-based governance regimes (EU AI Act-style) are shown to be fragile, motivating the reporting of grader confidence intervals and consensus rather than point estimates at decision boundaries. This reduces the chance that a genuinely unsafe model is deployed (or a safe one needlessly blocked) purely because one arbitrary grader configuration happened to fall on the permissive side of the line.

# Strength Rationale

Strong on narrow scope and low compute: a tightly-bounded multi-judge simulation with a single headline deliverable (flip-probability vs threshold), all inference-only on open judges with no proprietary data. Counterfactual value is high because it is directly policy-relevant governance work that labs underinvest in and that an independent team can do entirely externally within the time budget.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'release-gate robustness report': for a given benchmark and threshold, the probability that grader choice alone flips the verdict, plus a consensus-reporting fix.
