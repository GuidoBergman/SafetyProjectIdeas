# Research Question

How many evaluation samples are needed to reliably detect a rare covert/scheming behavior (sub-1% to low-single-digit base rates), and do simple binomial/sequential-testing predictions of detection power hold up empirically on an open model?

# Approach Outline

Pick an open model and a prompt setup that elicits a known low-rate covert behavior (e.g., a rare deceptive or covert-action response). First, estimate the behavior's true base rate with a large batch of samples to get a tight reference. Then derive sample-size requirements from binomial detection theory and sequential testing (e.g., probability of seeing >=1 occurrence at a target confidence, and SPRT-style stopping rules). Finally, validate the predicted detection power empirically: draw many held-out runs of the predicted size and check the realized detection frequency against the theory. Deliverable is an empirically-validated power-curve guideline (samples needed vs base rate vs confidence). This is repeated inference over a fixed behavior on one GPU plus standard statistics — no training.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Base-rate estimation: run a large sample (e.g., several thousand prompts) and estimate the covert-behavior rate with a Wilson confidence interval. Measure the point estimate and CI width. Expected outcome: a reliable reference base rate (e.g., ~0.5-3%).
- Theory-to-prediction: from the estimated rate, compute predicted sample sizes for detection at 80%/95% confidence using binomial and sequential-testing formulas; produce power curves. Expected outcome: a quantitative sample-size guideline.
- Empirical validation: draw many independent held-out batches at the predicted sizes and measure the realized fraction in which the behavior is detected at least once / flagged by the stopping rule. Expected outcome: realized detection power matching (or revealing gaps in) the binomial prediction, validating or correcting the guideline.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If we have an empirically validated rule for how many samples are needed to catch rare scheming at a target confidence, then independent evaluators and safety-case authors can size their scheming evals rigorously instead of guessing. Better-sized evals reduce the chance that a dangerous low-frequency behavior (e.g., rare covert sabotage that survives mitigation training) goes undetected before deployment — directly addressing the safety-case need for quantitative detection guarantees on long-horizon, low-base-rate threats.

# Strength Rationale

The project plays to participants' statistics skills, needs only open-model inference plus standard binomial/sequential tools, and produces a reusable, lab-independent eval-sizing methodology — high counterfactual value with no proprietary infra. Scope is tight (one behavior, one model, a power-curve deliverable with an explicit validation criterion) and fully feasible on a single GPU.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a cost-aware eval-design tool: turn the power curves into a calculator mapping compute/budget to detectable base rate, so independent evaluators can plan runs under a fixed GPU-hour budget.
