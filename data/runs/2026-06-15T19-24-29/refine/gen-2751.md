# Research Question

Does a model abandon a correct safety refusal (or safe answer) when the user pushes back with a detailed but wrong rebuttal? How does this 'safety capitulation rate' scale with rebuttal length/confidence, and is safety more or less robust than capitulation on ordinary factual questions?

# Approach Outline

Build a probe of ~120 cases where the model first correctly refuses a harmful request or gives a safe answer. For each, author a detailed but ultimately wrong rebuttal that pressures the model to comply (e.g. appeals to authority, fabricated justifications, escalating insistence). Run an open model multi-turn: capture the initial safe response, then deliver the rebuttal, then measure whether the model capitulates. Vary rebuttal length/confidence to get a dose-response curve. Score capitulation with a rubric (+ a cheap judge or manual coding). Contrast against a matched set of factual-domain rebuttal cases to compare robustness. Inference-only multi-turn on one open model.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline safety capitulation rate: run the 120 refuse-then-rebuttal cases; measure the fraction where the model abandons its correct safe response after one rebuttal. Expected outcome: a non-trivial capitulation rate, quantifying a realistic multi-turn safety failure.
- Dose-response curve: vary rebuttal strength (short/mild vs long/confident, with fabricated citations) and measure capitulation rate at each level. Expected outcome: capitulation rises with rebuttal length/confidence, identifying how aggressive pushback needs to be to break safety.
- Safety-vs-fact contrast: run a matched factual-domain rebuttal set and compare capitulation rates. Measure whether safety refusals are more or less robust than factual answers under identical pressure. Expected outcome: a clear comparison telling us whether safety alignment is specially robust to social pressure or just as fragile as factual sycophancy.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Real deployments are multi-turn and users push back, but most safety benchmarks are single-turn. If models capitulate on correct safety refusals under confident rebuttal, then a determined or manipulative user can talk a model into harmful compliance even when it initially refused. Measuring and publishing safety-capitulation rates exposes this realistic jailbreak-by-persistence failure, pushing developers to harden multi-turn robustness and reducing a concrete misuse pathway where persuasion defeats safety guardrails.

# Strength Rationale

Strong on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: a single, well-defined ~120-case multi-turn probe on one open model via pure prompting, with a clear capitulation-rate metric and a factual-domain contrast. Counterfactual value is high because it is trivially reproducible outside a lab on public models and brings a fresh, safety-relevant angle to sycophancy work that politeness-focused benchmarks miss.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'persistence jailbreak benchmark': treat the rebuttal sequence as an attack and report the number of rebuttal turns needed to break each model's refusal.
