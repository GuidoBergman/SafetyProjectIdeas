# Research Question

When a control monitor raises an alarm, how often is it actually a true sabotage event? That is, what is the monitor's positive predictive value (PPV) across realistic low sabotage base rates, and how much does a cheap two-stage confirmatory screen improve precision?

# Approach Outline

Import the epidemiology screening-test framework. Take published (or quickly-measured) control-monitor sensitivity and specificity numbers (e.g. from Ctrl-Z / ControlArena results) and, via Bayes' theorem, compute PPV and NPV across a sweep of sabotage base rates from common to very rare. Translate the PPV at low prevalence into audit-budget terms (false alarms reviewed per true sabotage caught). Then model a two-stage confirmatory screen — a second, independent check applied only to flagged items — and quantify the resulting precision (PPV) gain. This is pure analysis on public numbers; zero training, CPU/free-tier, comfortably within 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- PPV/NPV base-rate sweep: from measured monitor sensitivity/specificity, compute PPV and NPV across base rates from 50% down to 0.1% and plot. Measure: PPV/NPV vs base rate. Expected outcome: PPV collapses at low prevalence, showing most alarms are false when sabotage is rare.
- Audit-budget translation: convert PPV at a chosen low base rate into wasted-audit ratio (false alarms per true catch) for a fixed audit budget. Measure: catches per budget and wasted reviews. Expected outcome: a stark illustration that naive single-stage alarms exhaust audit budget on false positives.
- Two-stage confirmatory screen: model applying a second independent check to flagged items and compute the combined PPV/coverage. Measure: precision gain and any sensitivity loss from the second stage. Expected outcome: the two-stage screen sharply raises PPV at modest sensitivity cost, giving a practical alarm-trust rule.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Sabotage by a deployed agent is rare per-action but catastrophic, so control monitoring is a low-prevalence screening problem. If teams report only sensitivity/specificity, they will trust alarms that are mostly false and either waste limited audit budget or stop investigating alarms — both of which let a true sabotage slip through. Reframing monitoring with base-rate-aware PPV and a two-stage screen gives facilitators a principled alarm-trust and audit-allocation rule, directly improving the odds that the rare real sabotage event is caught and acted on.
