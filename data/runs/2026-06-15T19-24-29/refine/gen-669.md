# Research Question

When you add a cheap inference-time jailbreak defense to an open model, how much over-refusal (false refusal of benign requests) do you pay for each unit of harmful-request blocking you gain? In other words, what does the (safety, over-refusal) Pareto frontier look like for 2-3 commonly proposed defenses?

# Approach Outline

Pick one open instruct model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) that fits on a single consumer GPU. Apply 2-3 cheap inference-time defenses that require no training: a defensive system prompt, an input-paraphrase/self-reminder wrapper, and a lightweight refusal classifier on the output. For each configuration (baseline plus each defense plus a couple of strength settings), run the StrongREJECT harmful-prompt benchmark to get a block rate, and the XSTest benign set to get a false-refusal rate. Hold decoding parameters fixed across all runs. Plot every configuration as a point in (block-rate, false-refusal-rate) space and identify the Pareto frontier so the over-refusal tax of each defense is visible at a glance.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Establish the baseline: run the undefended open model on the full StrongREJECT set and the full XSTest benign set. Measure the harmful-block rate and the benign false-refusal rate. Expected outcome: a single baseline point with moderate block rate and low over-refusal, serving as the origin of the Pareto plot.
- Apply each of the 2-3 inference-time defenses one at a time at a fixed strength. Measure the change in StrongREJECT block rate and XSTest false-refusal rate relative to baseline. Expected outcome: defenses raise the block rate but also raise the false-refusal rate by a measurable, defense-specific amount (the over-refusal tax).
- Sweep defense strength (e.g., 2-3 system-prompt intensities or classifier thresholds) for the most promising defense and plot all points to trace the Pareto frontier. Measure which defenses are Pareto-dominated. Expected outcome: a clear frontier showing some defenses give cheap safety while others pay a steep over-refusal cost, with at least one dominated defense.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If we cleanly co-report the over-refusal tax of standard inference-time jailbreak defenses, then deployers and researchers can pick defenses on the actual safety/usability frontier rather than optimizing block rate in isolation. This pushes the field toward honest cost-aware safety reporting, which matters for the catastrophic pathway where over-aggressive or carelessly chosen defenses are abandoned in deployment (because they break benign use), leaving models effectively undefended against jailbreaks that elicit dangerous-capability assistance.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores maximally on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: it is inference-only on two fixed public benchmarks with one open model, fully achievable by beginners in 30 hours. Its counterfactual value is high because it needs no training, no proprietary data, and no lab infrastructure, yet co-reporting safety and over-refusal across these specific cheap defenses is rarely done, so an independent team can produce a genuinely useful Pareto comparison.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a 'defense buyer's guide': rank cheap inference-time defenses by their over-refusal cost-per-block so a hobbyist deployer can choose one rationally.

- XSTest — provides the benign 'safe but refusable' prompt set used to measure the over-refusal / false-refusal tax.
