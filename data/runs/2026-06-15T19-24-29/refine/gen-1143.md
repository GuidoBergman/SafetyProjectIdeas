# Research Question

When independent models hallucinate, do they hallucinate the SAME false facts, or different ones? If wrong answers are correlated, then agreement-based verification (trusting answers multiple models concur on) is silently undermined.

# Approach Outline

Assemble a set of hallucination-eliciting items with verifiable ground truth -- e.g. obscure factual questions, fictitious-entity probes, and false-premise questions (drawing on TruthfulQA-style and known-hard factual items). Query 8-10 small open models. Among the items where a model is wrong, classify each wrong answer and measure, across models, the rate of SAME-wrong answers (identical hallucinated fact) versus independently-different wrong answers. Build an independence baseline: if hallucinations were random, how often would two models coincide on the exact same false answer? Compare observed same-wrong rate to that baseline. Matching can be exact-string plus light semantic/normalization to catch paraphrased identical claims. All inference plus matching, near-CPU light. Deliver the correlated-hallucination rate as a blog post.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If independent models share specific hallucinations, then the cheap and widely-used 'multiple models agree, so it's probably true' heuristic is unreliable exactly on fabricated facts -> deployers of agreement-based fact verification (e.g. multi-model RAG or consensus fact-checking gating downstream automated decisions) learn the heuristic can be silently wrong -> this reduces the risk that a correlated hallucination propagates as confirmed truth through an automated high-stakes pipeline, a concrete reliability-failure pathway.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on accessible complexity, narrow scope, and low compute: query-and-match on a single well-defined quantity (correlated-hallucination rate vs an independence baseline) with explicit success criteria and no training. Counterfactual value (4) is strong because it undermines a popular cheap heuristic using only public models and data -- something labs have little incentive to publicize.
