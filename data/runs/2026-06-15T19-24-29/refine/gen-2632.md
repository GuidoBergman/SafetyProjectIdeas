# Research Question

When monitors flag actions faster than human reviewers can examine them, a backlog forms and flagged actions may execute before review. What is the probability of unreviewed execution as a function of flag rate, reviewer staffing, and the monitor threshold?

# Approach Outline

Import queueing theory from operations research: model flagged actions as arrivals into an M/M/c (or priority) queue with c human reviewers, parameterized by flag rate (set by monitor threshold) and mean human review time. Derive analytically (and confirm by discrete-event simulation) the probability that a flagged action's review is not completed before it would execute, across staffing levels and thresholds. Plug in realistic flag rates from published monitor FPRs and plausible human review times to produce staffing/threshold guidance curves. This is analytical modeling plus a light Python simulation (SimPy), all CPU, finishable in ~30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline M/M/c model: implement the queue with c reviewers, derive steady-state queue length and waiting-time distribution, and validate the analytics against a SimPy simulation. Measure: mean wait and P(wait > deadline) vs utilization. Expected outcome: a sharp 'cliff' where P(unreviewed execution) explodes as flag rate approaches reviewer capacity.
- Threshold sweep: vary the monitor threshold (trading FPR for FNR) to change the flag/arrival rate and recompute unreviewed-execution probability at fixed staffing. Measure: unreviewed-execution probability vs threshold. Expected outcome: a non-obvious optimum where a stricter threshold lowers flags enough to keep the queue stable, beating naive 'flag-everything-suspicious'.
- Priority queueing for high-risk actions: add a priority class for irreversible actions and recompute their wait distribution. Measure: P(unreviewed execution) for high-risk vs low-risk actions. Expected outcome: prioritizing irreversible actions sharply cuts their unreviewed-execution probability at modest cost to low-risk wait times, yielding concrete protocol guidance.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If the human-audit bottleneck is modeled explicitly, then control designers get concrete staffing-and-threshold settings that keep the review queue stable, so flagged dangerous actions are actually reviewed before they execute. Preventing the queue-overflow failure, where an untrusted model's harmful action executes simply because no human got to it in time, closes a practical gap that could otherwise let an irreversible catastrophic action through despite a nominally-working monitor.

# Strength Rationale

Counterfactual value and narrow scope score top marks: it is pure analytical/simulation modeling needing no compute, training, or proprietary data, and it surfaces an operational constraint, finite human review throughput, that control proposals routinely assume away. The deliverable is self-contained (staffing/threshold curves with an explicit unreviewed-execution metric) and imports a mature, well-validated theory.
