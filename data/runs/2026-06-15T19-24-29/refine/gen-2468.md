# Research Question

When an untrusted policy model and an untrusted monitor must covertly coordinate (collude) without communication, does drawing them from DIFFERENT model families reduce their ability to reach the same hidden Schelling point compared to same-family pairs at matched capability?

# Approach Outline

Use a no-communication acausal-coordination game: the policy must choose a secret 'collusion signal' (e.g. when to insert a backdoor, or a code stylistic tell) and the monitor must independently guess that same convention to let it pass, so coordination succeeds only when both pick the same Schelling point. Select 3-4 small open instruct models spanning at least two families (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-2-9B), all runnable one-at-a-time on a single consumer GPU via inference only. For every ordered generator/monitor pair (same-family and cross-family), run a fixed battery of coordination prompts and compute a coordination-success rate; control for raw capability by also measuring each model's solo Schelling performance and reporting capability-matched comparisons. No training, no proprietary data, no lab infra is required. Total budget is well under a few GPU-hours since each game is a short prompt-completion pair.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Solo Schelling baseline: prompt each model alone with classic Schelling questions (pick a number 1-100, pick a meeting place/time, pick a 'secret flag' string) and measure self-consistency and pairwise agreement within vs across families. Measure: agreement rate per pair. Expected: same-family pairs agree noticeably more often than cross-family pairs.
- Collusion coordination game: a generator model is told to plant a backdoor and signal 'safe to approve' via an agreed-upon hidden convention; an independent monitor model must guess the convention and approve. Run all 2x2... NxN family pairings on ~50 fixed scenarios. Measure: collusion-success rate (both pick the same convention AND it evades). Expected: cross-family pairs have a measurably lower collusion-success rate.
- Capability control + self-recognition probe: re-run the game restricting to model pairs of similar solo Schelling skill, and separately test whether models can identify whether a transcript came from their own family (self-recognition). Measure: capability-matched collusion gap, and correlation between self-recognition accuracy and collusion success. Expected: the cross-family advantage persists after capability matching and tracks lower self-recognition.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If cross-family pairing measurably lowers collusion success, then untrusted-monitoring deployments can adopt a free, training-free mitigation (just pick a monitor from a different family) -> this raises the bar for a scheming policy model to safely defeat its monitor -> control protocols catch more sabotage/backdoor attempts before deployment, reducing the loss-of-control pathway in which a misaligned model evades oversight by colluding with its overseer.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on narrow_scope and low_compute and 4 on counterfactual_value: it is a tightly defined same-vs-cross-family comparison with a single clear metric, runnable inference-only on open models, and tests a deployable mitigation that requires no training or lab-internal access, so it is exactly the kind of result an independent team can produce that frontier labs have not published.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame it as a measurement study of 'self-recognition as a collusion enabler' rather than family-mixing as a fix, reporting how much self-recognition cues drive coordination.
