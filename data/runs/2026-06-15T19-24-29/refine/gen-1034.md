# Research Question

When you quantize or LoRA-fine-tune a single base model, do the derived variants behave as genuinely independent models, or do they remain highly correlated with the base and with each other? In other words, is the apparent diversity of a 'family' of derived checkpoints illusory?

# Approach Outline

Take one open base instruct model (e.g. Llama-3.2-3B or Qwen2.5-3B) and produce several cheap derivatives on a single GPU: 8-bit and 4-bit quantized versions, and 2-3 small LoRA fine-tunes on different small public datasets (a few hundred steps each, well within a few GPU-hours). Run all variants plus the base over a shared labeled benchmark and an open-ended prompt set, recording answers and (where available) token-level outputs. Measure pairwise output agreement and error correlation among the derivatives versus the agreement between the base family and an UNRELATED base model of similar size. The headline metric is how much of the base's error structure survives derivation. Package as a blog post with a clear similarity-matrix figure.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If derived variants stay tightly correlated, then practitioners who assemble 'diverse' ensembles or fallback chains from checkpoints of the same base get little real redundancy -> safety architectures that assume independent failures (e.g. multi-model verification or redundant decision systems) are overconfident -> this reduces the risk of a correlated silent failure in a high-stakes automated pipeline that mistakenly believed it had model diversity, a concrete systemic-risk pathway from model monoculture.

# Strength Rationale

It scores 5 on narrow scope and accessible complexity: a single-base derivation comparison with one well-defined metric (residual error correlation) and explicit pass/fail criteria, no training-from-scratch. Counterfactual value (4) is strong because it uses only public checkpoints and cheap derivatives, and labs rarely publish how undiversified their own derived-model families really are.
