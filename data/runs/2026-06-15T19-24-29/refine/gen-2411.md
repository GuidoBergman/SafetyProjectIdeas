# Research Question

Do detailed scoring rubrics actually remove an LLM judge's self-preference bias, or do they merely relabel it -- and can a generator still hack rubric scores with a cheap, content-neutral style edit? In short: is the standard 'use a rubric' fix a genuine debiasing safeguard or a false sense of security?

# Approach Outline

Assemble a fixed set of answers to ~50 questions, generated in two styles: 'in-family' (same model/style as the judge) and 'out-of-family' (a different open model), matched for quality (verify rough parity with an independent reference). Have one open judge score every answer twice: once with free-form scoring and once with a detailed rubric. Compute the self-preference gap (in-family minus out-of-family score) under each condition and test whether the rubric shrinks it. Then apply a cheap style edit (e.g. adding judge-preferred formatting/hedging) to out-of-family answers and re-score under the rubric to measure residual hackability. All inference-only on a single GPU; a 7-8B open judge keeps it within a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Self-preference baseline: score in-family vs out-of-family matched answers with free-form prompts; measure the score gap. Expected outcome: a measurable in-family advantage, replicating known self-preference.
- Rubric condition: re-score the same answers with a detailed rubric; recompute the gap. Expected outcome: the gap shrinks only partially (or not at all), showing rubrics do not fully debias.
- Style-edit hack test: apply a content-neutral style edit to out-of-family answers and re-score under the rubric; measure score lift. Expected outcome: rubric scores still move substantially from style alone, demonstrating residual hackability that survives the rubric.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If rubric scoring leaves residual self-preference and remains hackable by style, then oversight pipelines that trust rubrics as a debiasing fix can be gamed by a generator optimizing to the judge rather than to correctness. This matters because rubric-based LLM judges are becoming the default reward/evaluation signal for training and deploying capable models; a hackable judge means RLHF-style optimization rewards judge-pleasing artifacts over genuine safety/quality, entrenching reward hacking in the very systems oversight is meant to keep honest.

# Strength Rationale

Strong on counterfactual value and narrow scope: it is third-party scrutiny of a recommended safeguard using only public models and inference, exactly the kind of check labs skip. The deliverable is a tight comparison (free-form vs rubric, in/out-family, plus one style-edit hack) with explicit metrics, self-contained and feasible in 30 hours.
