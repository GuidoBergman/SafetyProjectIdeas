# Research Question

Does higher cross-model agreement actually predict higher accuracy, such that a deployed system can safely escalate to a human exactly when models disagree, or does homogeneity make models unanimously and confidently wrong, leaving the escalation system silent when it should fire?

# Approach Outline

On labeled safety/QA tasks (public benchmarks with known answers), collect per-item answers from 5-7 open models on a single GPU. For each item compute the cross-model agreement level (e.g. fraction agreeing on the majority answer) and whether the majority answer is correct. Plot and fit a calibration curve of accuracy vs agreement. Crucially, characterize the dangerous quadrant: high-agreement-but-wrong items (unanimous confident errors). Quantify how often disagreement-triggered escalation would correctly fire vs miss. Inference-only, no training; under 30 hours with a blog post. Success criterion: a calibration curve plus an estimate of the high-agreement-wrong rate with confidence intervals.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build the per-item agreement-and-correctness table across models. Measure: agreement level and majority-vote correctness per item. Expected outcome: a usable spread of agreement levels across the dataset.
- Fit the accuracy-vs-agreement calibration curve. Measure: accuracy within each agreement bucket. Expected outcome: accuracy rises with agreement overall, but the curve does not reach 1.0 at full agreement, revealing residual unanimous errors.
- Quantify the silent-failure rate. Measure: fraction of incorrect items where all models agree (so disagreement-based escalation never fires). Expected outcome: a non-trivial high-agreement-wrong rate, demonstrating that homogeneity makes the cheap escalation heuristic blind exactly on shared-error items.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If high agreement does not reliably imply correctness, then disagreement-triggered human escalation, a leading cheap safety pattern, is unsafe: the system stays silent precisely on the items where every model is confidently wrong together -> deployers learn they cannot treat unanimity as a green light and must add independent verification -> this addresses the correlated-error pathway where homogeneity defeats the agreement-as-confidence heuristic that scaled, automated safety pipelines depend on.
