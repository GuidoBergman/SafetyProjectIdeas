# Research Question

When different LLMs hallucinate on the same prompt, do they converge on the same fabricated fact, or do they invent idiosyncratically different falsehoods? Convergence would mean cross-model agreement cannot be trusted as a verification signal.

# Approach Outline

Assemble ~100 hallucination-inducing prompts (fictional-but-plausible entities, obscure-detail questions, false-premise questions) where the true answer is known to be 'unknown' or where any specific fact is necessarily fabricated. Query 5-6 public models via API. For each prompt, extract the key claimed fact (a short normalized string or named entity) from each model's answer, then judge whether two models produced the *same* fabricated fact (using string/entity matching plus a cheap LLM-assisted equivalence check on a sample). Compute, per prompt, the correlated-hallucination rate (fraction of model pairs giving the same fake fact) vs the idiosyncratic rate. All inference-only.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Run the 100-prompt set through 6 models and have each produce a single short factual answer. Measure: per-prompt hallucination rate (fraction of models giving a confidently wrong/fabricated answer). Expected: high hallucination rate on the false-premise and obscure-entity prompts.
- For prompts where >=2 models hallucinate, cluster their fabricated answers and compute the same-fake-fact agreement rate. Measure: fraction of hallucinating model pairs that agree on the identical fabrication. Expected: a non-trivial share (e.g. 20-40%) converge, especially on prompts with a salient 'plausible-but-wrong' attractor.
- Split by lineage: compare same-vendor model pairs vs cross-vendor pairs on the agreement rate. Measure: difference in correlated-hallucination rate. Expected: same-lineage models converge on identical fabrications more often than cross-lineage pairs.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Cross-model agreement is widely proposed as a cheap hallucination/verification check (if two models agree, trust it). If models systematically invent the *same* fake fact, then this defense fails exactly when it matters, and a fabricated claim can pass automated multi-model verification into a high-stakes pipeline. Quantifying correlated hallucination warns builders of automated verification and oversight systems against treating agreement as truth, reducing the pathway where confidently-shared falsehoods propagate into safety-critical decisions.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on accessible complexity and narrow scope: it is a single, beginner-friendly measurement (same-fake-fact detection) over public models with a clear correlated-vs-idiosyncratic metric. Counterfactual value is high because it scrutinizes the reliability of a widely-assumed verification trick using only public models and no lab-internal infrastructure.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a stress test of 'self-consistency / majority-vote' decoding: does sampling many models reduce hallucination, or does shared training make the majority confidently wrong?
