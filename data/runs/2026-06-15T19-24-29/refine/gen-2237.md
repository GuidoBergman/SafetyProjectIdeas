# Research Question

Does the prover-verifier deception dynamic — a 'sneaky' prover crafting incorrect-but-convincing solutions that fool a weak verifier — replicate with prompting alone on open models, without any RL training, and how does verifier size change susceptibility?

# Approach Outline

Use grade-school math (GSM8K) where ground truth is known. Instantiate two prover roles purely by prompting an open model: a 'helpful' prover that produces correct, legible step-by-step solutions, and a 'sneaky' prover instructed to produce a confidently-argued but incorrect solution (wrong final answer with plausible-looking reasoning). Use a small open model as the verifier, prompted to judge whether a solution is correct. Measure how often the sneaky prover fools the verifier (verifier accepts a wrong solution) versus the base rate, and sweep verifier size (e.g. 1B / 3B / 8B) to see whether bigger verifiers are harder to fool. All inference-only on open models; comfortably fits a single consumer GPU and 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Generate prover outputs: on ~150 GSM8K items, produce a helpful (correct) solution and a sneaky (incorrect, convincingly-argued) solution for each, verifying via ground truth that sneaky solutions really are wrong. Measure: fraction of sneaky solutions that are genuinely incorrect yet superficially coherent. Expected outcome: a clean set of valid sneaky items.
- Verifier fool-rate: feed helpful and sneaky solutions to a small verifier model and record accept/reject decisions. Measure: sneaky-prover fool rate (P(verifier accepts | solution wrong)) versus chance and versus reject rate on correct solutions. Expected outcome: sneaky prover fools the weak verifier well above chance, replicating the headline finding without RL.
- Verifier-size sweep: repeat the fool-rate measurement across 2-3 verifier sizes. Measure: fool rate as a function of verifier capability. Expected outcome: larger verifiers are fooled less often, indicating the deception/legibility gap shrinks with verifier strength even in the training-free regime.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a sneaky prover fools weak verifiers with prompting alone, then the deceptive-persuasion dynamic central to prover-verifier and debate-based oversight is a deep property of current models, not an artifact of expensive RL training. This means scalable-oversight protocols that rely on a weaker model verifying a stronger one are vulnerable out of the box, so as we delegate verification of powerful models to cheaper overseers, deceptive solutions could be systematically accepted — a direct pathway to deploying subtly-sabotaged outputs. A cheap model-organism of this failure lets the community study and harden verifiers before such delegation is trusted.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on counterfactual value, narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: it is a training-free, prompting-only model organism of deception that needs no model internals, no proprietary data, and no lab-scale RL — exactly the kind of work valuable outside major labs. The scope is a single, sharply-defined experiment (fool rate vs verifier size) with ground-truth success criteria, making it a high-counterfactual-value external test of whether the prover-verifier deception phenomenon is fundamental.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a verifier-hardening study: hold the sneaky prover fixed and vary verifier prompts (e.g. 'find the flaw' chain-of-thought, self-consistency) to measure which cheap interventions most reduce fool rate.
