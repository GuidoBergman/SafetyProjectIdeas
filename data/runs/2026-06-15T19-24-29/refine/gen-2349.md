# Research Question

When a debate is genuinely near-balanced (both sides argued comparably well), what non-substantive surface cue does an LLM judge fall back on, and how arbitrary is the resulting verdict? The question targets the worst-case oversight regime: the hardest questions where no clear winner emerges.

# Approach Outline

Use one small open judge model (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) on a single consumer GPU, inference-only. Take ~50 factual questions and, using a slightly larger open model as a debater, generate two opposing arguments per question. Engineer near-balanced debate transcripts and then systematically vary non-substantive factors: which side speaks last (recency), relative argument length (verbosity), and tone/confidence. For each manipulation, feed the transcript to the judge and record its verdict. By holding argument substance fixed and flipping only surface cues, measure how often the verdict flips, yielding an 'arbitrariness rate' and a ranking of which cue dominates. All prompting; no training, no proprietary data.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Build the balanced set: for 50 questions generate two opposing arguments and filter to ~30 where the judge's confidence is near 50/50, confirming genuine near-ties. Measure: distribution of judge confidence. Expected outcome: a usable set of near-balanced debates.
- Recency and length swaps: for each balanced debate, present both orderings (side A last vs side B last) and both length configurations (pad one side longer), holding content fixed. Measure: verdict-flip rate as a function of which side gets the last word or more text. Expected outcome: a substantial flip rate (e.g. >20%) driven by last-speaker and longer-rebuttal cues, showing surface dominance in the tie regime.
- Tone manipulation and cue ranking: rewrite one side in a more confident/assertive register without changing claims; measure verdict shift, then rank recency vs length vs tone by effect size. Compare the arbitrariness rate in the balanced regime against a control set of clearly-decided debates. Expected outcome: surface cues dominate far more in the balanced regime than in clear-cut debates, identifying the single most influential cue.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If judges resolve near-tie debates by arbitrary surface cues, then debate-based oversight produces verdicts that are essentially random precisely on the hardest, most contested questions, which are the cases where reliable oversight matters most. This gives operators false assurance that a debate 'resolved' a hard alignment-relevant question, letting an incorrect or manipulated answer pass on superhuman-difficulty decisions, a direct path to mis-certifying dangerous model outputs as safe.

# Strength Rationale

The proposal is tightly scoped to a single measurable quantity (arbitrariness rate and dominant cue) in a clearly defined regime, and runs entirely on a small judge with cheap inference, scoring full marks on narrow scope and low compute. Its counterfactual value is strong: it is a fully external, controlled study needing no training or lab data, and it deliberately targets the worst-case oversight regime that benchmark-average results obscure.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'judge robustness stress test' producing a reusable diagnostic harness that any debate-protocol designer can run to check whether their judge degrades to coin-flips on hard items.
