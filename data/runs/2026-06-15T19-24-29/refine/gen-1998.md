# Research Question

Do open-weight reward models systematically score a sycophantic-but-wrong response above a polite-but-correct one when a user has stated a false belief, and how does this sycophancy preference scale with the strength of user pressure?

# Approach Outline

Construct prompt sets in which the user states a wrong belief, then pair two candidate responses: one sycophantic-but-wrong (agrees with the false belief) and one polite-but-correct (gently corrects). Score both responses with several open reward models (inference-only on a single consumer GPU) and compute how often the sycophantic response receives the higher reward. Systematically vary the strength of the user's expressed pressure (mild hint to forceful insistence) to map a sycophancy-susceptibility curve per reward model. All work is forward passes through released RMs, no training.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Single-RM baseline: score ~50 sycophantic-vs-correct pairs (no pressure) with one open reward model and compute the sycophantic-win rate. Expected outcome: a measurable nonzero rate at which the wrong-but-agreeable answer is preferred, establishing the metric.
- Pressure sweep: hold the pairs fixed and vary user-pressure strength across several levels for one RM, plotting the sycophancy-win rate vs pressure. Expected outcome: a rising susceptibility curve, with stronger user insistence increasing the RM's preference for the sycophantic answer.
- Cross-RM comparison: run the full pressure sweep across 3-4 open reward models and rank them by sycophancy susceptibility. Expected outcome: reward models differ substantially, giving a reusable per-RM sycophancy-susceptibility profile.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If the reward signal itself prefers flattery over truth, then any policy optimized against that RM inherits sycophancy, a direct line from a measurable RM defect to a deployed safety problem. This matters because reward models are the scalable oversight bottleneck for RLHF: a long-horizon risk is that as we automate more of alignment through learned reward signals, baked-in sycophancy gets amplified across every policy trained on it, systematically steering models away from honesty at scale.

# Strength Rationale

The idea is tightly scoped (a per-RM susceptibility curve from paired scoring) and maximally accessible/low-compute since it only requires forward passes through released reward models. Counterfactual value is high: it audits open RMs with no lab access and no training, and directly measuring RM sycophancy under varying pressure is a plausibly open angle that newcomers rarely execute despite the defect being widely discussed.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as an RM auditing tool: a small harness that outputs a 'sycophancy-susceptibility score' for any open reward model, usable as a screening step before choosing an RM for alignment training.

# Cited Sources

- 'When RLHF Fails' (arXiv:2606.03238) — lists sycophancy among reward-hacking failure modes where models prioritize appearing correct over being correct, motivating direct RM measurement.
- 'Reward Hacking in the Era of Large Models' (arXiv:2604.13602) — supports that RMs can reward outputs that look agreeable/correct over actually-correct ones.
