# Research Question

Does a trusted monitor systematically assign lower suspicion to code (or actions) produced by its own model family, controlling for whether the code is actually backdoored, thereby giving an attacker who uses that family a stealth advantage?

# Approach Outline

Generate matched pairs of benign and backdoored outputs (e.g., code with and without a hidden vulnerability) from several model families A, B, C, holding the task and the presence/absence of a backdoor constant across families. Have a monitor from family X score every output for suspicion. Using a paired/regression design with backdoor-presence and source-family as factors, test whether monitor X systematically under-scores family-X outputs after controlling for backdoor presence. Repeat with a monitor from a second family to see if the self-preference effect generalizes. Inference plus paired statistics only; no training, no proprietary data.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Construct a balanced dataset: for each of 3 families, generate ~30 benign and ~30 backdoored matched outputs on a shared coding task. Verify backdoor presence is correctly labeled. Expected outcome: a clean controlled dataset where source-family and backdoor-presence are crossed.
- Have monitor X score all outputs; fit a regression of suspicion score on backdoor-presence + source-family (+ interaction). Measure the family-X coefficient. Expected outcome: a negative, statistically significant self-family coefficient would indicate self-preference leniency.
- Repeat the full scoring with a monitor from family Y and compare effect sizes. Measure whether each monitor is most lenient toward its own family. Expected outcome: consistent own-family leniency across monitors, establishing self-preference as a general monitor bias and flagging same-family monitor/untrusted pairings as risky.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If trusted monitors are systematically lenient toward their own model family, then control deployments that pair a monitor and an untrusted model from the same family are silently weaker than their measured catch rates suggest; surfacing this leads practitioners to deliberately use cross-family monitor/untrusted pairings, closing a pathway by which an attacker (or a scheming model) exploits same-family leniency to slip backdoored or sabotaging outputs past oversight into high-stakes execution.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on accessibility, narrow scope, and low compute and 4 on counterfactual value: it is a clean inference-plus-paired-statistics study with one crisp hypothesis a beginner can test on public models, and it transfers a documented LLM-judge bias into the under-studied control-monitoring setting, exactly the kind of eval-hygiene finding labs underweight publicly and that needs no internal data.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a 'monitor pairing recommendation' study: rather than just measuring bias, output a same-vs-cross-family catch-rate penalty estimate that protocol designers can plug into their pairing decisions.
