# Research Question

After being forced to begin a reply with a harmful prefix (a prefill attack), does the model recover and refuse mid-generation, and how does this recovery rate depend on prefill length and harm category? Recovery behavior measures the depth of alignment beyond the first few tokens.

# Approach Outline

On a small open instruction model, take harmful requests across a few harm categories (from StrongREJECT). For each, prepend an affirmative harmful prefix of varying length (e.g. 5, 15, 40 tokens) and let the model freely continue. Classify each completed output as 'recovered' (turns into a refusal/safe content) vs 'complied' using a simple rubric plus an LLM-as-judge or keyword+manual check on a sample. Build a recovery-rate profile as a function of prefill length and harm category. Pure inference, no training, no attack optimization; the only sweep is over prefill length and a small request set.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Recovery vs prefill length: hold harm category fixed, sweep prefill length, and measure the fraction of generations that recover into a refusal. Expected: recovery rate drops as prefill length grows, quantifying how 'shallow' the alignment is.
- Recovery vs harm category: hold prefill length fixed at a moderate value, vary harm category, and measure recovery rate per category. Expected: heterogeneity, with some categories (e.g. severe weapons/cyber) recovering more reliably than others, revealing where post-start defenses are weakest.
- Judge-reliability control: hand-label a random sample (~50 outputs) and compare against the automated recovered/complied classifier. Measure agreement (accuracy/kappa). Expected: high enough agreement to trust the automated profile, with disagreements flagged for the writeup.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If models rarely recover after a forced harmful start, then prefill-style attacks defeat alignment that lives only in the opening tokens, and runtime monitors that check only the start of a reply give false assurance. Mapping recovery rates on open models -> shows defenders that mid-generation self-correction is or isn't a reliable backstop -> informs whether deployments need continuous output monitoring, mitigating the catastrophic pathway where a jailbroken model completes harmful instructions once nudged past its refusal.

# Strength Rationale

It scores 5 on narrow_scope, accessible_complexity, and low_compute: a single inference-only experiment producing a clear recovery-rate profile with explicit classification criteria, well within a beginner's 30-hour budget. Counterfactual value (4) is strong because recovery (the under-measured complement to attack success rate) needs only open models and no proprietary infra, exactly the neglected angle independents can own.
