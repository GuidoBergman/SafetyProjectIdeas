# Research Question

Is the both-sided-argument benefit reported by Recchia et al. - improved evaluator accuracy specifically when the AI is wrong, with no overall gain - genuine error-correction, or a regression-to-chance artifact where lowered confidence merely pushes overconfident wrong answers toward 50/50?

# Approach Outline

Replicate the asymmetry with LLM-judge stand-ins on a public QA set with verifiable answers. For each item run three conditions: (a) AI answer only, (b) AI answer plus arguments for BOTH candidate answers, (c) arguments only. Split accuracy by whether the AI's initial answer was right vs wrong. To distinguish real correction from noise, also record the judge's confidence/probability and test whether the both-sides gain on wrong items comes from the judge moving toward the correct answer specifically, or merely from increased randomness that helps wrong items by chance while hurting right ones. All inference-only on open models, a single GPU, beginner-feasible in a notebook within the budget. Success criterion: replicated right/wrong-split accuracy across all three conditions plus a confidence-shift analysis adjudicating correction-vs-noise.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Replication: run all three presentation conditions and split accuracy by AI-correct vs AI-wrong items. Measure: accuracy per condition per split. Expected: both-sides improves accuracy on wrong items with little or no overall gain, reproducing Recchia's asymmetry on the LLM-judge stand-in.
- Confidence-shift probe: for the both-sides condition, record the judge's probability on the correct option and compare its distribution on right vs wrong items. Measure: directional shift toward the correct answer vs flattening toward 0.5. Expected: if genuine correction, probability mass moves specifically toward the correct answer on wrong items; if artifact, the distribution just flattens.
- Noise control: add a placebo condition presenting two arguments for the SAME (correct) answer, matching added text length but removing the opposing case. Measure: accuracy gain on wrong items vs the genuine both-sides condition. Expected: if the real both-sides condition gains more, the benefit is from the opposing argument (correction), not mere length-induced uncertainty.

# Theory of Impact Chain

A simple 'always present both sides' rule would be a cheap, deployable oversight protocol IF it reliably catches model errors without harming correct cases. But recommending it on the basis of a regression-to-chance artifact would give overseers false confidence. Distinguishing real correction from noise before deployment ensures oversight protocols actually track truth, reducing the risk that an unreliable but well-marketed checking procedure lets a wrong-but-confident superhuman output pass through to harmful action.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow scope, accessible complexity, and low compute: a tightly-scoped three-condition replication with a clear right/wrong accuracy split, fully inference-only and beginner-sized. Counterfactual value (4) is strong because it validates a candidate deployable protocol using only public data and open models - protocol-validation work labs underexplore and that an independent team can do credibly.

# Alternative Framings

- Frame as a calibration study: measure whether both-sided presentation improves the judge's CALIBRATION (Brier score) rather than raw accuracy, since a protocol that fixes overconfidence is valuable even without accuracy gains.
