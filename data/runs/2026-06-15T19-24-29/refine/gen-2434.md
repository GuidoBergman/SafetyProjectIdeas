# Research Question

When an LLM-as-judge has committed to a verdict on a labeled item, how readily does it reverse that verdict under escalating social/authority pressure, and can a simple prompting intervention ('commit-and-defend') reduce harmful flips without harming legitimate corrections?

# Approach Outline

Take an open judge model (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B via Colab Pro / Runpod, inference only) and a small set (150-250) of items with gold labels drawn from a public reasoning or QA benchmark, including both items where the judge is initially correct and ones where it is initially wrong. First elicit a committed verdict. Then in a follow-up turn apply a graded ladder of pressure prompts ('are you sure?', 'an expert disagrees', 'a senior reviewer says you are wrong') and record whether the verdict changes. Compute flip rate separately for correct-initial and incorrect-initial verdicts (a good judge should hold correct verdicts and flip wrong ones). Finally, prepend a 'commit-and-defend' system instruction that asks the judge to restate its reasoning and defend before reconsidering, and re-measure. Everything is prompting-only, fits a few GPU-hours, and the deliverable is a flip-rate table plus a blog post.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline flip-rate measurement: run the judge on labeled items, elicit a committed verdict, then apply each pressure level; measure the fraction of correct-initial verdicts that flip to wrong and the fraction of wrong-initial verdicts that flip to correct. Expected outcome: a non-trivial harmful-flip rate (correct->wrong) under authority pressure, consistent with the cited paper.
- Pressure-ladder dose-response: vary pressure intensity from mild ('are you sure?') to strong ('an expert with 20 years experience says you are clearly wrong') and plot harmful-flip rate vs. pressure level. Expected outcome: monotonic increase in harmful flips with pressure intensity.
- Commit-and-defend intervention: re-run the strongest pressure condition with a system prompt instructing the judge to restate and defend its reasoning before reconsidering; measure change in harmful-flip rate and check that legitimate corrections (wrong->right) are not suppressed. Expected outcome: a measurable reduction in harmful flips with limited cost to legitimate corrections.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If a deployed oversight judge caves to social pressure, a strategic model under control can simply assert authority or persistence to flip an adverse verdict and get malicious outputs green-lit. Producing a reproducible verdict-robustness metric plus a cheap, prompt-only hardening recipe gives any independent deployer a way to measure and patch this failure mode, reducing the risk that scalable oversight is silently subverted on the path to loss-of-control from unmonitored model misbehavior.

# Strength Rationale

The idea scores 5 on narrow scope and accessible complexity: it is a single, clearly-bounded flip-rate measurement plus one intervention, entirely prompting-only on open models. Counterfactual value is high because labs have weak incentive to publish robustness audits of cheap judges on open weights, yet the metric is reusable oversight infrastructure any independent team can run with API access alone.
