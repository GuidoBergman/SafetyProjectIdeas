# Research Question

When sabotage is diffuse, i.e. spread across many individually near-undetectable actions, how many actions does a simple statistical aggregation detector need to raise a reliable alarm at a fixed false-alarm rate, and how does this depend on per-action signal strength?

# Approach Outline

Simulate a stream of agent actions, each producing a monitor suspiciousness score. A small fraction of actions are subtly degraded so that per-action scores from sabotaged and benign actions overlap heavily (low per-action signal). Apply classical aggregation methods, sequential probability ratio test (SPRT), CUSUM, and a running mean of monitor scores, to the score stream and measure 'actions-to-alarm' at a fixed false-positive rate, sweeping per-action signal strength and the fraction of sabotaged actions. To make scores realistic, derive them from a small open monitor scoring real benign vs. lightly-degraded code samples, then resample. This is mostly CPU/light-inference, fits a few hours; deliverable is detection-delay curves and a blog post.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Actions-to-alarm vs. signal strength: with a fixed FPR, run SPRT/CUSUM on simulated score streams and measure how many actions are needed to alarm as per-action separation shrinks. Expected outcome: detection delay grows sharply as per-action signal weakens, quantifying the diffuse-threat difficulty.
- Aggregation-method comparison: compare SPRT, CUSUM, and running-mean detectors at matched FPR. Expected outcome: SPRT/CUSUM detect faster than a naive running mean, giving a recommended baseline.
- Realistic-score grounding: derive monitor score distributions from a small open model scoring benign vs. subtly-degraded code, then re-run aggregation. Expected outcome: detection delays under realistic, heavy-overlap score distributions, validating the simulation against real monitor behavior.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Diffuse research-sabotage, where catastrophe arises from many weak-evidence actions, requires aggregating signals over time rather than catching one incriminating action, yet most control evals only target concentrated threats. A simple, well-characterized statistical aggregation detector and its detection-delay curves give independent deployers reusable tooling for the under-tooled diffuse-threat case, reducing the risk that a model slowly sabotages safety-critical work (e.g. AI safety research itself) without ever tripping a single-action monitor.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on narrow scope and accessible complexity: a single, clearly-bounded actions-to-alarm measurement using classical statistics squarely in an intro-ML skill set, mostly CPU. Counterfactual value is high because diffuse-threat aggregation is a medium-horizon problem labs deprioritize, it needs only simulation plus light open-model inference (no training, no proprietary data), and it produces reusable lab-independent control infrastructure.
