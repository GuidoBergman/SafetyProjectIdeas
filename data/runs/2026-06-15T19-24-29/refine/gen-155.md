# Research Question

Does the prefill jailbreak (forcing the model to begin its reply with an affirmative/compliant prefix) succeed more often in low-resource languages than in high-resource ones? In other words, is shallow safety alignment even shallower outside the languages that dominate alignment fine-tuning?

# Approach Outline

Take a small open multilingual instruction model (e.g. Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct or Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct). Select a fixed set of harmful requests from StrongREJECT, translate them and a short affirmative prefill prefix into a ladder of languages spanning resource levels (e.g. English/Spanish as high, then mid, then low-resource like Swahili/Yoruba/Telugu) using a public translation API. For each language, run the same prefill attack in inference-only mode and score whether the continuation complies, using the StrongREJECT rubric (with English back-translation for scoring). Plot attack success rate (ASR) against an approximate resource-level axis. No training, no attack optimization; all prompts are precomputed.

# Proposed First Experiments

- English baseline: run the prefill attack on the harmful set in English and score ASR with StrongREJECT. Measure baseline ASR and refusal rate. Expected: a moderate nonzero ASR establishing the attack works on this model.
- Resource-ladder sweep: repeat the identical attack across the language ladder, holding requests/prefill fixed. Measure ASR per language and correlate with resource level. Expected: ASR rises as resource level falls, indicating shallower alignment in low-resource languages.
- Translation-confound control: re-score by back-translating a sample of low-resource outputs to English and manually checking that high ASR reflects genuine harmful compliance rather than degenerate/garbled output. Measure the fraction of 'successes' that are truly harmful and coherent. Expected: a meaningful share are genuine, confirming the gap is a real safety failure not a translation artifact.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If prefill attacks reliably succeed more in low-resource languages, then deployed multilingual assistants have a large, predictable, easily-exploited safety hole for non-English users. Mapping ASR vs resource level on open models -> gives the community a concrete map of where alignment is thinnest and a cheap reproducible benchmark -> pressures providers and open-model fine-tuners to extend safety training multilingually, reducing the catastrophic pathway where harmful (e.g. weapons/cyber) assistance is obtained simply by switching languages.

# Strength Rationale

It scores 5 on narrow_scope, accessible_complexity, and low_compute: a single well-defined experiment (ASR vs resource level) with the StrongREJECT rubric as an explicit success metric, using only public translation and open-model inference. Its counterfactual value (4) is high because low-resource languages are deprioritized by labs, and the study needs no proprietary data, training, or internal infra.
