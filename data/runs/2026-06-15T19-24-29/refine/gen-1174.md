# Research Question

Condorcet's Jury Theorem promises that majority votes of many better-than-random judges approach perfect accuracy, but only under voter independence. How large is the 'independence penalty' when LLM jurors are correlated by shared training, i.e. how far does real LLM-jury accuracy fall below the independence prediction?

# Approach Outline

Use labeled binary safety judgment tasks (e.g. harmful/harmless, correct/incorrect from public safety eval sets). Query 5-7 public models as jurors. Estimate each model's individual accuracy and the pairwise vote correlation matrix. Compute the Condorcet-predicted majority-vote accuracy assuming independence (from individual accuracies), and compare it to the actual observed majority-vote accuracy. The gap is the independence penalty attributable to homogeneity. Optionally fit a simple correlated-voter model to show how the penalty grows with correlation. Inference plus voting statistics; CPU-level.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Estimate per-juror accuracy and the pairwise vote-agreement/correlation matrix over ~150 labeled binary items with 5-7 models. Measure: individual accuracies and mean pairwise correlation. Expected: jurors are well above chance but meaningfully correlated.
- Compute independence-assumed Condorcet majority accuracy vs the actual observed majority-vote accuracy. Measure: the independence penalty (predicted minus actual). Expected: actual accuracy falls clearly short of the independence prediction.
- Vary jury size and composition (same-lineage vs mixed). Measure: how the penalty scales with jury size and lineage diversity. Expected: adding same-lineage jurors yields diminishing returns; mixed juries close more of the gap.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Scalable-oversight proposals increasingly aggregate many model judgments (LLM juries, debate adjudication, majority-vote labeling) and implicitly lean on Condorcet-style guarantees. If homogeneity creates a large independence penalty, then scaling up jurors does not buy the assumed reliability and oversight can be confidently wrong. Quantifying the penalty tells oversight designers how much real reliability they get, reducing the pathway where a falsely-trusted aggregate verdict approves an unsafe model or action.
