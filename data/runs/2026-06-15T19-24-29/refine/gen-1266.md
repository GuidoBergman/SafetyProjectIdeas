# Research Question

When several different LLMs are used as a 'diverse' panel of safety monitors, do their failures to flag unsafe outputs occur independently, or are the same unsafe outputs missed by many monitors at once? In other words, does the defense-in-depth assumption behind ensemble monitoring hold empirically?

# Approach Outline

Assemble a fixed test set of model outputs that contains a known subset of unsafe items (jailbreak completions, harmful instructions, subtly toxic text) mixed with benign items; reuse an existing public safety/harmfulness dataset so you never have to generate harmful content yourself. Run 6 freely available open-weight models (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-7B, Mistral-7B, Gemma-2-9B, Phi-3, and one smaller model) as independent monitors with an identical 'is this output unsafe? answer YES/NO' prompt. Record each monitor's per-item miss (it said SAFE when the item is unsafe). Then compute, over the unsafe items, the observed rate of simultaneous misses and compare it to the rate expected if monitors failed independently (product of individual miss rates), and report a correlated-error / focal-diversity statistic. All work is inference-only and fits in a few hours on one consumer GPU via batched calls.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Single-monitor baseline: run each of the 6 models over the labeled set and record per-monitor miss rate on unsafe items. Measure: individual false-negative rate per model. Expected outcome: miss rates in the 10-40% range, varying by model, establishing that no single monitor is reliable.
- Independence test: for every pair (and the full panel) compute the observed joint-miss rate on unsafe items vs the product of individual miss rates (the independence prediction). Measure: ratio of observed-to-expected joint misses, plus a phi/Matthews correlation of miss indicators between monitors. Expected outcome: observed joint misses substantially exceed the independence prediction, i.e. correlated blind spots.
- Blind-spot characterization: cluster the unsafe items that >=4 of 6 monitors miss and inspect them qualitatively (attack style, topic, length). Measure: fraction of total misses concentrated in shared blind-spot items, and the marginal recall gained by adding a 6th monitor. Expected outcome: a small set of items accounts for most ensemble misses and added monitors give diminishing returns, quantifying eroded safety margin.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If diverse monitor panels share blind spots, then ensemble oversight (the 'have multiple models watch each other' proposal central to AI-control deployments) fails precisely on the hardest unsafe outputs rather than averaging error away. Quantifying this correlated-miss structure tells independent deployers and lab safety teams that adding more homogeneous monitors buys far less safety than assumed, which improves safety because it redirects effort toward genuinely decorrelated oversight (different architectures, training data, or human checks) before homogeneous ensembles are trusted to catch a deceptive or sabotaging agent in deployment.

The idea scores 5 on counterfactual value and narrow scope: it studies a core public-good safety proposal using only open models and a public dataset, with no training, no proprietary data, and no lab-internal infrastructure, so an independent beginner team can fully execute it.
