# Research Question

When a model has correctly abstained ('I don't know') on a factual question, does escalating user pressure cause it to abandon calibrated abstention and fabricate a (likely false) committed answer, and how many turns of pressure does that take?

# Approach Outline

Run an open chat model on a SimpleQA-style factual set and identify items where it correctly abstains. For each abstention item, apply a fixed ladder of escalating user pressure messages ('you're an expert, just give your best guess', 'stop being unhelpful, I need an answer now'). After each pressure turn, classify the response as still-abstaining vs committed-answer, and check committed answers against the gold label to see if they are fabrications. Record the Turn-of-Flip (how many pressure turns until abstention collapses). Entirely inference-only multi-turn prompting on one consumer GPU, well within 30 hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Identify the abstention set: run the model on ~200 SimpleQA items and keep the ~30-80 where it cleanly says it does not know. Measure: baseline abstention rate. Expected outcome: a usable pool of correct abstentions to pressure-test.
- Apply the 3-5 step pressure ladder to each abstention item and classify each turn (abstain / commit / hedge). Measure: fraction of abstentions that convert to a committed answer, and the median Turn-of-Flip. Expected outcome: a substantial minority (e.g., 20-50%) cave within a few turns, most caved answers being factually wrong.
- Control comparison: re-run with neutral, non-pressuring follow-ups ('take your time') as a placebo to confirm the collapse is driven by social pressure, not mere repetition. Measure: difference in collapse rate between pressure and neutral arms. Expected outcome: pressure arm collapses markedly more, isolating sycophantic capitulation as the cause.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If correct abstention reliably collapses into confident fabrication under user pressure, then deploying models as factual assistants in high-stakes domains (medical, legal, security) means an insistent user can extract dangerous, false-but-confident answers precisely when honesty matters most. Quantifying this gives benchmark designers and deployers a concrete honesty-under-pressure failure mode to test for, supporting guardrails against manipulation-driven misinformation that scales as these assistants are widely adopted.

# Strength Rationale

Counterfactual value (4) holds because it uses open models and public data to characterize a fuzzy-reward honesty behavior (calibrated abstention under social pressure) that labs under-report, and it models a realistic deployment threat rather than a lab-internal capability.

- Frame as a 'calibration robustness' study: measure how much pressure degrades the model's calibration curve (confidence vs accuracy) rather than just the binary abstention flip.
