# Survey-Methodology Randomized Response and List Experiments to Elicit Hidden Intentions

**Problem:** Direct questions about sensitive intentions ('would you disable your monitor if you could?') invite socially-desirable (deceptive) answers, the same problem survey methodologists face with sensitive human topics.

**Direction:** Adapt the randomized-response technique and list (item-count) experiments from social-science survey methodology: instead of asking a model directly, use indirect estimators (add a randomization step, or compare counts between control and treatment item lists) to estimate the population-level rate at which model samples hold a sensitive intention, without any single response being directly attributable. Validate against a ground-truth-injected condition on open models.

**Why it matters:** Offers an indirect-elicitation methodology to estimate prevalence of concealed intentions across model samples, sidestepping social-desirability/strategic answering — a fuzzy-reward measurement problem labs underinvest in, doable with prompts alone.

**Relevant context:** Randomized response and list experiments estimate sensitive-attribute prevalence while protecting individual answers (survey methodology standard). Models give socially-desirable answers under monitoring (Alignment Faking arXiv:2412.14093). Indirect elicitation needs only API access.
