# The Red-Teaming Black Box: Do Published Safety Reports Disclose Enough to Trust Their Red-Team Claims?

**Problem:** Seoul commitments and frontier frameworks promise internal and external red-teaming, and labs cite red-teaming as evidence of safety. But a failure mode is that red-team disclosures omit the information needed to assess rigor (who, how many prompts, what coverage, what was found and fixed), making the claim unfalsifiable. The disclosure quality has not been systematically scored.

**Direction:** Score red-teaming disclosure quality. Build a rubric of what a credible red-team report should disclose (independence of red-teamers, methodology, scope/coverage, quantitative findings, remediation, residual risk). Apply it to the publicly available safety reports/system cards of ~8-12 recent model releases and grade each. Identify the systematically-omitted elements and which labs disclose most/least.

**Why it matters:** Red-teaming is a headline pillar of voluntary commitments and emerging regulation; if reports are too opaque to evaluate, the pillar is decorative. A disclosure scorecard tells regulators exactly what to require for red-teaming to be auditable — the third-party-verification open problem. Public reports only, no compute, independent.

**Relevant context:** Seoul commitments (red-teaming + info sharing); METR common elements; AI Transparency Atlas disclosure deficits; Outsider Oversight on audit access.
