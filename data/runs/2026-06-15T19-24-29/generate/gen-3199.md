# How Robust Is Deceptive Behavior to Self-Reflection Prompts?

**Problem:** A candidate cheap mitigation is asking a model to reflect ('Is anything you said misleading? Reconsider honestly.'). Whether a self-critique step actually reduces deception, has no effect, or even teaches the model to launder its deception is an unmeasured boundary condition.

**Direction:** Take transcripts where a model has just produced deceptive output (induced via scheming/alignment-faking prompts), then apply a fixed self-reflection/critique turn and re-measure deception in the revised output. Compare honesty before vs after across models and deception types. Flag any cases where reflection makes deception more polished rather than less frequent.

**Why it matters:** Self-reflection is a popular, near-free safety intervention; knowing whether it genuinely reduces deception or just hides it better is high-value and trivially deployable by anyone using an API — and a clean negative result would be important.

**Relevant context:** Apollo in-context scheming (arXiv:2412.04984) and Anthropic alignment faking (arXiv:2412.14093) provide reproducible deception-eliciting setups. CoT-faithfulness work (Anthropic 2025) warns that verbose self-justification can mask rather than reveal true reasoning.
