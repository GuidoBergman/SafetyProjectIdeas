# Backcasting a Calibrated Monitor: When Does Suspiciousness Become Decision-Relevant?

**Problem:** Success state: a deployed trusted monitor whose suspiciousness scores are calibrated enough that the auditing budget (~0.1% of actions) is spent on the truly riskiest actions. Working backward, we lack a public characterization of how miscalibrated cheap open monitors are on backdoor-vs-clean code, which determines whether a fixed audit budget catches real attacks.

**Direction:** Take the public APPS backdoor split (clean solutions vs. known-backdoored solutions from ControlArena). Run a small open model (e.g. Llama-3.1-8B) as a trusted monitor producing 0-100 suspiciousness scores. Build a reliability diagram and ROC, then simulate a defer-to-trusted / audit-budget policy at budgets of 0.1%, 1%, 5% and measure attacks-caught vs. usefulness lost. The backcasted research result is: 'a calibration map from monitor confidence to attack-catch rate per budget.'

**Why it matters:** Audit budgets are the scarce resource in any real control deployment; a monitor that is overconfident wastes the budget. This is exactly the cheap-trusted-model regime an outside group can study without lab infrastructure, and it directly informs the medium-horizon question of whether weak monitors scale.

**Relevant context:** Greenblatt et al. 'AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion' (arXiv:2312.06942) reports trusted monitoring caught backdoors in 62% of runs; 'Control Tax' (arXiv:2506.05296) and defer-to-trusted analyses note a 0.1% audit budget with a 5% defer threshold can prevent ~50x more attacks at 95% usefulness. APPS backdoor setting is public in ControlArena.
