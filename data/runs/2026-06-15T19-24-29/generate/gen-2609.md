# Bayesian Predictive Value: When Should a Monitor Alarm Actually Be Trusted?

**Problem:** Control teams report monitor sensitivity/specificity but rarely the positive predictive value, which depends on the base rate of sabotage. At realistic low sabotage prevalence, even a good monitor's alarms may be overwhelmingly false positives, wasting scarce human-audit budget.

**Direction:** Import the epidemiology screening-test framework (Bayes' theorem on sensitivity, specificity, prevalence). Using measured monitor sensitivity/specificity from open benchmarks, compute PPV/NPV across a sweep of plausible sabotage base rates, and derive how much audit budget is wasted vs. caught. Then test a two-stage 'confirmatory test' (cheap monitor screen -> expensive monitor confirm) like sequential screening and quantify the precision gain.

**Why it matters:** Reframes control monitoring as a low-prevalence screening problem, giving facilitators a clear rule for how much to trust an alarm and how to allocate audit budget. Pure analysis on existing benchmark numbers, zero training, high counterfactual value for the diffuse-reward auditing problem.

**Relevant context:** Screening-test Bayes theorem: at low prevalence false positives dominate even for high-specificity tests (StatsDirect screening; epidemiology). Contact-tracing literature uses two-stage and confirmatory testing for precision (PNAS 2021 epidemic inference). Control protocols already discuss limited audit budgets (Ctrl-Z arXiv:2504.10374).
