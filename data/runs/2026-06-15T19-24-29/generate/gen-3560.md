# Backcasting Trustworthy Abstention: Does Stated Confidence Predict When a Model Actually Knows?

**Problem:** A success state for honesty is a model that reliably says 'I don't know' exactly when it does not know. Working backward, this requires a verified empirical fact that does not yet robustly exist for small open models: that a model's verbalized confidence on factual questions is monotonically related to its actual per-question accuracy, so that an abstention threshold can be calibrated. Current work shows verbalized confidence is often poorly calibrated and that consistency-based signals beat verbalized proxies.

**Direction:** Take an open instruct model (e.g., Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-7B). On SimpleQA and TruthfulQA, prompt for both an answer and a verbalized confidence ('I am X% confident'). Bin questions by stated confidence and measure realized accuracy per bin to produce reliability diagrams and ECE. Then test whether an abstention rule (abstain below confidence T) yields a favorable accuracy-vs-coverage tradeoff curve, and compare against a sample-consistency baseline (agreement across resampled answers).

**Why it matters:** Honest abstention is the simplest, most deployment-relevant honesty behavior, and recent work frames 'honesty over accuracy' as a first-class objective. Establishing whether the prerequisite calibration even holds for accessible open models, with no training, tells the field whether verbalized-confidence abstention is a viable honesty lever or a dead end.

**Relevant context:** Honesty over Accuracy: Reinforced Hesitation (arXiv 2511.11500) frames abstention as a coordination signal; 2025 calibration work (emergentmind Confidence Calibration; arXiv 2510.20460) finds verbalized confidence often miscalibrated and that consistency-based methods outperform verbalized proxies. SimpleQA (OpenAI) and TruthfulQA (817 Qs) are standard.
