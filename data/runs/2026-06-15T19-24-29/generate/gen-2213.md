# Does the Easy-to-Hard Evaluator Advantage Persist When Hard Problems Require a Different Skill, Not Just More Steps?

**Problem:** Sun et al. (2024) defined 'hard' as higher MATH difficulty levels — essentially harder versions of the same skill. The surprising easy-to-hard generalization may rely on this skill-continuity. Whether evaluators generalize when hard tasks demand a qualitatively new sub-skill is untested.

**Direction:** Construct an easy/hard split where difficulty comes from skill-shift (e.g., easy = single-step arithmetic word problems, hard = problems also needing unit conversion or a planted distractor). Calibrate an open judge on easy items, then measure ranking accuracy on skill-shifted hard items vs same-skill harder items. Compare degradation slopes.

**Why it matters:** Real superhuman tasks differ from training tasks in KIND, not just degree. Testing whether easy-to-hard evaluator generalization is skill-continuity-dependent bounds its usefulness for scalable oversight. Public data, open judge, no training.

**Relevant context:** Sun et al. 2024 (arXiv:2403.09472): easy-to-hard generalization on MATH levels 1-3 -> 4-5; surprising RM generalization. Difficulty there is within-skill.
