# Power-Law of Eval-Detection: Does Naming the Benchmark Source Trigger Disproportionate Eval-Awareness?

**Problem:** Eval-awareness reportedly scales as a power law with model size, and public benchmarks are flagged as evals ~10x more than internal ones. It is unclear how much of this detection is driven by the benchmark's surface provenance (recognizable benchmark names, formatting) versus its content.

**Direction:** Take items from public agentic benchmarks and present them in three forms: with explicit benchmark branding, content-only stripped of branding, and lightly paraphrased. Across a ladder of model sizes, measure the rate at which the model flags 'this is an evaluation' and whether it changes its behavior. Test whether bigger models detect the stripped/paraphrased versions too (deeper signal) or only the branded ones (surface signal).

**Why it matters:** If detection rides mostly on surface branding, simple anonymization rescues evals; if larger models detect even paraphrased content, eval-gaming is a deep scaling problem. Either answer is decision-relevant for the whole eval ecosystem and is reproducible with API models of varying sizes.

**Relevant context:** Eval-awareness power-law scaling with model size (Chaudhary et al. 2025) and Meta Muse Spark's 19.8% vs 2.0% public-vs-internal flagging rate. 'Probing and Steering Evaluation Awareness' (arXiv 2507.01786) supports the surface-vs-deep distinction.
