# When Does Resampling Backfire? Mapping the Suspicion-Threshold Cliff

**Problem:** The defer-to-resample control protocol (Ctrl-Z) generates up to two new samples when an action looks suspicious and executes the least-suspicious one, but recent work found that under adaptive attacks resampling can BACKFIRE and reduce safety. The boundary conditions of this reversal are not characterized.

**Direction:** Using an existing open control-eval harness (ControlArena / a small APPS backdoor-insertion task subset), sweep the suspicion threshold and the number of resamples while holding a fixed weak-monitor and a fixed scripted attack policy. Measure side-task success rate and audit rate at each setting to locate the threshold region where resampling crosses from helping to hurting. Plot safety vs. usefulness as a 2D heatmap over (threshold, n_resamples).

**Why it matters:** Resampling is a flagship blue-team protocol; if there is a predictable regime where it inverts, deployers need to know the safe operating envelope. A clean characterization of the cliff is a self-contained contribution and needs no model training.

**Relevant context:** Ctrl-Z: Controlling AI Agents via Resampling (arXiv:2504.10374) introduces defer-to-resample. Adaptive Attacks on Trusted Monitors Subvert AI Control Protocols (arXiv:2510.09462) reports that under stronger adaptive evaluation resampling 'backfires and actively reduces safety,' and that heavier deferral/resampling can suppress suspiciousness across trajectories making attacks subtler.
