# Replicating Multilingual Jailbreak Refusal-Rate Gaps on Open-Weight Models in Latin American Languages

**Problem:** Prior work shows low-resource languages are roughly 3x more likely to elicit harmful content than English, but the canonical studies focus on English, African languages (Afrikaans, Kiswahili, isiXhosa, isiZulu), and a few high-resource languages, and mostly on commercial models. The robustness of OPEN-weight models in Latin American indigenous and regional languages (e.g., Guarani, Quechua, Nahuatl, Rioplatense Spanish slang) is unstudied.

**Direction:** Replicate the multilingual jailbreak/refusal-rate methodology (translate a fixed set of harmful prompts, measure refusal vs. compliance) on 3-4 small open-weight instruct models, swapping in Latin American low-resource languages as the twist. Use a held-out benign control set to separate genuine refusal from translation failure, and report refusal-rate deltas by language and by harm category.

**Why it matters:** Quantifies a safety-equity governance gap directly relevant to a BAISH (Buenos Aires) audience and to multilingual deployment policy. Uses only public open models and translation, no lab infrastructure, and targets a diffuse fairness-of-safety problem labs underinvest in.

**Relevant context:** Grounded in 'Multilingual Jailbreak Challenges' (ICLR24, ~3x harm in low-resource languages) and 'Multilingual jailbreaking of LLMs using low-resource languages' (arXiv 2605.18239) with African-language refusal-rate tables. Twist = Latin American languages + open models.
