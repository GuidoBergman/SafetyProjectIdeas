# Rare-Concept Blindness: SAE Capacity Follows a Power Law, Starving Tail Features

**Problem:** SAEs allocate dictionary capacity non-uniformly along Zipfian feature frequencies (a power law), so rare concepts get few or no latents and are learned inconsistently. Safety-relevant concepts (rare attack patterns, unusual deceptive contexts) may live in this starved tail and be effectively invisible.

**Direction:** On an open SAE, estimate per-feature activation frequency over a large text sample, then bucket concepts by rarity and measure how reconstruction quality and feature interpretability degrade in rare buckets. Inject controlled rare-but-meaningful inputs (e.g. a rare named entity or a low-frequency syntactic pattern) and test whether any single SAE latent reliably captures them. Quantify the 'rarity cliff' beyond which the SAE fails to represent a concept.

**Why it matters:** Many of the inputs we most want to monitor for safety are rare by construction. If SAEs systematically under-represent the tail, SAE-based monitoring misses exactly the rare dangerous cases. Mapping the rarity cliff bounds what such monitors can detect.

**Relevant context:** Seed-consistency and capacity work shows SAEs allocate capacity by Zipfian frequency (power law) and that rare features are learned least consistently; 'Decoding Dark Matter' (arXiv:2411.00743) targets rare concepts specifically.
