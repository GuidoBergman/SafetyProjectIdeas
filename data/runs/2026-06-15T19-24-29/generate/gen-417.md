# Is the simplicity bias real in superposition? Testing whether networks pick the lowest-complexity interpolant on a controlled task

**Problem:** Theory of inductive biases holds that among many interpolating solutions, networks favor 'simpler' ones, and this simplicity bias is invoked to argue both for and against alignment-relevant generalization. Direct, controlled measurement of which interpolant a small net selects — when simple and complex solutions are equally available — is still surprisingly underexplored by newcomers.

**Direction:** Design a tiny classification task with a deliberately constructed simple feature (e.g. one informative coordinate) and a complex feature (a high-frequency combination) that BOTH perfectly fit the training data. Train small MLPs across seeds and measure, via held-out probes on each feature, which the network actually relies on. Vary width, depth, and weight decay to see how architecture nudges the bias.

**Why it matters:** Whether networks default to simple-but-correct or simple-but-spurious solutions is central to predicting OOD safety failures (goal misgeneralization, deceptive alignment arguments). A clean controlled measurement is exactly a theory-meets-experiment contribution doable in 30h on one GPU.

**Relevant context:** Grounded in the simplicity-bias / double-descent literature (arXiv:2403.10459, AF 'Understanding Deep Double Descent') and arXiv:2505.13230 on implicit bias producing scaling laws. Context open problem: 'theoretical study of inductive biases'.
