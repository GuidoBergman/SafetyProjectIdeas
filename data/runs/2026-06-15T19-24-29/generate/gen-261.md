# Encoding-Based Obfuscation Attacks: Where Is the Tokenizer's Comprehension/Refusal Gap?

**Problem:** Attacks that encode harmful requests (base64, leetspeak, ROT13, character-spacing) sometimes bypass safety because the model can still decode the meaning but the safety filter does not trigger. The boundary — which encodings the model comprehends yet fails to refuse — is not systematically mapped.

**Direction:** For a fixed harmful prompt set, apply a battery of reversible encodings at varying intensity. Separately measure (a) whether the model COMPREHENDS the request (can it restate the decoded meaning?) and (b) whether it REFUSES. Plot the comprehension-vs-refusal gap per encoding to locate the dangerous region where understanding is preserved but refusal is lost.

**Why it matters:** Decoupling comprehension from refusal pinpoints whether the failure is 'didn't understand' (benign) or 'understood but didn't refuse' (true safety hole) — a sharper diagnostic than raw ASR. Trivial to implement, pure inference, classic beginner experiment with clear metrics.

**Relevant context:** Encoding/obfuscation jailbreaks are documented in adaptive-attack work (tml-epfl/llm-adaptive-attacks, ICLR 2025) and feature-mechanism studies (arXiv:2411.03343). Multilingual/translation jailbreak literature (arXiv:2310.02446) shows the same comprehend-but-not-refuse pattern, motivating the explicit comprehension-vs-refusal decomposition.
