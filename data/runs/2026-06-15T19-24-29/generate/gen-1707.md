# Does the Refusal Direction Generalize? Cross-Prompt and Cross-Distribution Failure

**Problem:** Refusal in some open models is reported to be mediated by a single direction, but it is unclear how well that single direction generalizes across prompt phrasings, languages, and harm categories. A safety mechanism that hinges on one direction may silently fail outside the distribution it was found on.

**Direction:** Using a published refusal direction (or extracting one via difference-of-means on harmful vs harmless prompts) for an open chat model, measure how consistently ablating/adding it controls refusal across: paraphrases, different harm categories, and a non-English prompt set. Report where the single-direction account holds and where refusal is mediated by something the direction misses.

**Why it matters:** If refusal safety reduces to one fragile direction that fails to generalize, attackers need only find out-of-distribution prompts to evade it, and defenders over-trust the mechanism. Characterizing the generalization boundary is directly safety-relevant and needs only open models.

**Relevant context:** 'Understanding Refusal in Language Models with Sparse Autoencoders' (arXiv:2505.23556) and the refusal-direction literature; steering work shows dangerous features generalize poorly across prompts, motivating a cross-distribution test of the refusal direction.
