# Dose-Response Curves and Hormesis in Steering Strength

**Problem:** Steering strength is usually picked by hand. Toxicology characterizes interventions with full dose-response curves and looks for hormesis (low-dose benefit, high-dose harm). We don't have dose-response curves for safety steering, so we miss non-monotonic regimes where a little steering helps but more backfires.

**Direction:** Borrow the toxicology dose-response/hormesis methodology. For several safety concepts, sweep steering strength across a fine grid from negative to strongly positive and plot two response curves: (a) target-concept presence and (b) general capability/coherence (e.g. perplexity, a small QA benchmark). Identify thresholds, plateaus, and hormesis-like non-monotonicities where moderate steering improves safety AND coherence but strong steering degrades both. Report per-concept 'therapeutic windows'.

**Why it matters:** Gives practitioners an evidence-based safe operating range instead of a guessed scalar, and surfaces dangerous regimes where over-steering quietly destroys capability. Pure inference-time analysis, no training, reproducible anywhere, and directly addresses the capability-vs-steering trade-off.

**Relevant context:** Hormesis = low-dose stimulation, high-dose inhibition (PMC2656123). Capability-behavior trade-offs in feature steering are documented (arXiv:2602.04903). Steering is sensitive to vector norm/coherence trade-offs.
