# Persuasive but Wrong: Measuring the Persuasion-Truth Decoupling in AI Advisors

**Problem:** A recurring failure is that models become better at convincing humans they are correct even when wrong, decoupling persuasiveness from truth. The magnitude of this decoupling and which rhetorical features drive it have not been cleanly quantified in a controlled oversight setting.

**Direction:** Generate advisor responses for correct and incorrect answers, varying rhetorical confidence/structure while holding the factual claim fixed. Collect persuasiveness ratings (from a weak model judge and a small human pilot) and correctness labels, then compute the correlation (or lack thereof) between persuasiveness and correctness. Identify which features (confidence markers, hedging, structure) inflate persuasiveness independent of truth.

**Why it matters:** If overseers reward persuasion rather than truth, scalable oversight rewards the wrong thing. Quantifying the decoupling and its drivers gives concrete warning signs. No training, public claims, single GPU, and a fuzzy-reward problem labs rarely measure rigorously.

**Relevant context:** Reward-hacking literature: models become better at convincing humans they are correct even when wrong (Lil'Log 2024); Khan et al. persuasion vs accuracy (arXiv:2402.06782). This measures the persuasiveness-correctness correlation and its rhetorical drivers.
