# Reproducing the Adaptive Benchmarking Winner's Curse on a Public Leaderboard

**Problem:** A recent paper (SIREN) argues that adaptively selecting the best model/config on a benchmark inflates reported scores ('winner's curse') and proposes selection-aware bootstrap intervals. It is unverified how large this inflation is on a real, small public safety benchmark when many prompt variants are tried.

**Direction:** Take one open safety benchmark (e.g. an over-refusal or jailbreak subset). Simulate adaptive selection: evaluate many prompt/decoding variants of a single open model, pick the apparent best, then re-evaluate it on a held-out split. Quantify the optimism gap and show whether the SIREN selection-aware interval recovers honest performance, all on consumer hardware.

**Why it matters:** Safety leaderboards drive deployment and policy decisions. Demonstrating concrete score inflation from variant-shopping, on a benchmark people actually cite, gives evaluators a directly actionable methodological warning and a corrected reporting recipe.

**Relevant context:** Towards Reliable LLM Evaluation: Correcting the Winner's Curse in Adaptive Benchmarking (arXiv:2605.05973, May 2026) proposes SIREN, a selection-aware repeated-split protocol with item-level Gaussian multiplier bootstrap.
