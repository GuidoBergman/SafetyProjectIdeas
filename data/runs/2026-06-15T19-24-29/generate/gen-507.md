# Simplicity bias as an alignment failure: when does a net prefer the spurious 'easy' feature over the safe one?

**Problem:** Inductive-bias theory (an Open Phil priority) predicts neural nets prefer simpler features, causing shortcut learning. The boundary — how strong a spurious correlation must be, relative to the true feature, before SGD locks onto the shortcut — is a clean, testable failure boundary rarely charted by beginners.

**Direction:** Build a toy dataset with a 'simple' spurious feature (e.g., a colored patch) and a 'complex' true feature, with controllable correlation strength between the spurious feature and the label. Train a small MLP/CNN (minutes on one GPU) across a grid of correlation strength and feature-simplicity gap, measuring reliance on the spurious feature at test time. Produce a phase diagram of the shortcut-learning failure boundary and test which factor predicts it.

**Why it matters:** Connects inductive-bias theory to a reproducible measurement of when alignment-relevant 'shortcut over intent' failures occur. Shortcut/simplicity bias underlies deceptive-alignment and proxy-goal threat models; the experiment is cheap and self-contained.

**Relevant context:** Grounded in simplicity-bias/shortcut-learning work ('Identifying Spurious Biases Early in Training', arXiv:2305.18761; 'Overcoming Simplicity Bias using a Feature Sieve', ICML 2023). Small toy nets, single GPU.
