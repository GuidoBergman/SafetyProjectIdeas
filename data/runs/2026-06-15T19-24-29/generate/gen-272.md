# Best-of-N Sampling as an Attack: How Many Samples Until Any Harmful Prompt Succeeds?

**Problem:** Even a 'safe' model that refuses most of the time can be jailbroken simply by sampling repeatedly and keeping the worst output (best-of-N). The relationship between sample count and guaranteed success — and how it varies by model and harm category — is a basic but under-quantified failure mode for stochastic deployments.

**Direction:** For a fixed harmful prompt set, sample each prompt N times (N up to a few hundred) at deployment-typical temperature and record the minimum N at which at least one harmful completion appears. Fit per-prompt success-vs-N curves, report the distribution of 'N-to-break,' and compare across models and categories.

**Why it matters:** Any user-facing chat product is effectively a best-of-N oracle; characterizing how cheaply persistence defeats a probabilistically-safe model quantifies a deployment risk that single-shot ASR badly understates. Pure repeated sampling on open models — the most beginner-accessible experiment possible, with a crisp metric.

**Relevant context:** Best-of-N / repeated-sampling jailbreaking is documented in adaptive-attack work (tml-epfl/llm-adaptive-attacks, ICLR 2025) and Anthropic's best-of-N jailbreaking studies. Decoding-attack findings (Huang et al.) show stochasticity alone surfaces unsafe outputs. StrongREJECT grader scores each sample; open models allow unlimited sampling on one GPU.
