# Consequence Mapping: Does Safety Evaluation Score Predict Real-World Harm Incidents?

**Problem:** Consequential validity — whether evaluation scores actually predict the outcomes they claim to — is the most direct test of whether the safetywashing proxy-construct gap matters in practice. The under-studied link is whether there is any empirical relationship between a model's safety benchmark scores and documented real-world harm incidents or near-misses involving that model.

**Direction:** Assemble a dataset by cross-referencing: (a) published safety benchmark scores for named commercial models (available in benchmark papers and leaderboards), and (b) documented real-world AI harm incidents for the same models from harm incident databases (AIAAIC, MIT AI Risk Repository, or similar). Code incidents by harm type, severity, and model. Compute correlations between model safety scores and incident rates, controlling for deployment volume (more-deployed models will have more incidents by exposure alone). Test whether models that score highly on safety benchmarks have fewer/less severe incidents per deployment unit. This is a correlational study requiring data assembly, coding, and regression in R.

**Why it matters:** If benchmark safety scores do not predict real-world harm incidents, this is direct consequential validity evidence that the entire measurement system is broken. This is the most alarming possible finding and would motivate urgent reform. If they do predict, this provides the first empirical validation of safety evaluation.

**Relevant context:** The 'Measurement to Meaning' framework (arXiv 2505.10573) calls for consequential validity but provides no empirical test. AIAAIC and MIT AI Risk Repository provide harm incident data. The key methodological challenge is controlling for deployment volume — this is solvable with publicly available usage statistics (model release dates, API traffic estimates). No ML required.
