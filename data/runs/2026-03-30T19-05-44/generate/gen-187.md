# Item Exposure Control Analysis for AI Safety Benchmarks: Quantifying Data Leakage Risk Using Exposure Rate Diagnostics

**Problem:** AI safety benchmark items that are heavily exposed during training data collection inflate model performance beyond what genuine safety would predict—analogous to the CAT problem of overexposed items. There is no formal framework for quantifying which safety benchmark items are at highest contamination risk based on their characteristics.

**Direction:** Apply IRT-based item exposure analysis to AI safety benchmarks. Estimate item difficulty parameters using IRT. Identify items that are disproportionately 'overselected' by current models relative to their difficulty level—a pattern suggesting familiarity rather than safety generalization. Compare item difficulty-to-selection ratios against exposure rate thresholds used in CAT (e.g., Sympson-Hetter threshold of 0.5). Correlate exposure risk indicators with known data leakage signals (n-gram match with training corpora, semantic similarity to web-scraped safety datasets). Propose item rotation or item banking procedures analogous to CAT exposure control.

**Why it matters:** Item exposure control in CAT uses formal statistical methods to prevent test score inflation from item overexposure. AI benchmark contamination has been identified as a major threat to valid evaluation (Benchmark Contamination article). Applying formal exposure control diagnostics would provide a quantitative framework for contamination risk assessment that goes beyond simple n-gram matching. The methodology requires only IRT analysis and correlation in R.

**Relevant context:** IRT item exposure control literature is well-developed (Springer 2023, PMC6140306). AI benchmark contamination is documented and discussed as a major problem ('Benchmark Contamination: The AI Fraud Nobody Wants to Discuss'). N-gram overlap methods for contamination detection exist. The formal analogy between CAT item exposure and benchmark contamination is conceptually clear but appears not to have been developed in published work.
