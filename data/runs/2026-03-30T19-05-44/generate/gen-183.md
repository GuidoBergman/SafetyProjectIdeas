# Standardized Response Mean as a Responsiveness Index for AI Safety Benchmarks: Quantifying How Sensitively Benchmarks Detect Safety Improvements After Fine-Tuning

**Problem:** When an AI model undergoes safety fine-tuning, safety benchmarks should detect the improvement. But how sensitive are different benchmarks to genuine safety changes? A benchmark that cannot reliably detect a known safety intervention is not useful as a monitoring tool. Clinical measurement uses the Standardized Response Mean (SRM) to quantify this responsiveness.

**Direction:** Identify 3-5 model pairs where a base model and its safety-fine-tuned version are both publicly available (e.g., Llama base vs. Llama-chat, Mistral base vs. Mistral-Instruct). For each pair, compute safety scores before and after fine-tuning on multiple safety benchmarks. Compute the SRM (mean change / SD of changes) and Cohen's effect size (mean change / pooled SD) for each benchmark. Rank benchmarks by their SRM—higher SRM means the benchmark is more responsive to real safety improvements. Assess whether benchmark length, item diversity, or hazard category mix predicts responsiveness.

**Why it matters:** Responsiveness indices are the standard method in clinical outcomes research for evaluating whether a measurement instrument can detect meaningful change. If a safety benchmark has a very low SRM for known safety fine-tuning interventions, it cannot be used to monitor safety progress. This analysis could identify which benchmarks are actually useful for tracking safety improvements over time. The analysis requires only R and publicly available model responses.

**Relevant context:** SRM and Cohen's d are standard responsiveness measures in clinical outcomes research (PMC549031, ahajournals). Model pairs with safety fine-tuning variants are publicly available (Llama family, Mistral family). Multiple safety benchmarks with public leaderboards exist. This application of responsiveness methodology to compare the sensitivity of safety benchmarks appears novel.
