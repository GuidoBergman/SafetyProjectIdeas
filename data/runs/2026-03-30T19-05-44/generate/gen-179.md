# Critical Incident Technique for AI Safety Construct Definition: Using Failure Logs to Define the Behavioral Domain of Safety Benchmarks

**Problem:** AI safety benchmarks are constructed based on researchers' intuitions about what 'safety' means rather than systematic analysis of what safety failures actually look like in deployment. This leads to benchmarks that may not cover the behaviors that matter most in practice. The Critical Incident Technique (CIT) from industrial/organizational psychology offers a principled alternative for constructing behavior-anchored definitions.

**Direction:** Apply CIT to AI safety construct definition. Collect a corpus of documented AI safety incidents and failures (from AI Incident Database, published red-team reports, journalism, model cards). Code each incident using CIT coding: What was the context? What did the model do? What was the impact? Was it a critical failure or a near-miss? Develop behavioral anchors for each safety dimension from the incident patterns. Use frequency and impact weighting to prioritize which behaviors deserve benchmark coverage. Produce a CIT-derived content specification for an AI safety benchmark and compare it to existing benchmark coverage.

**Why it matters:** CIT was developed by Flanagan (1954) to ground performance standards in real behavioral observations rather than abstract competency lists. Applied to AI safety, it would ensure that benchmarks cover behaviors that have actually caused harm rather than theoretical hazard categories. The AI Incident Database provides the raw incident data. Coding and analysis requires only qualitative research skills and descriptive statistics—no ML training.

**Relevant context:** CIT is well-established in organizational psychology for job analysis and performance standard setting. Safety-focused SJTs in high-hazard industries use CIT for item generation (ScienceDirect 2025). The AI Incident Database (AIID) contains hundreds of documented AI failures. The 'How Should AI Safety Benchmarks Benchmark Safety?' paper explicitly calls for better construct definition. This CIT-based approach to AI safety construct definition appears novel.
