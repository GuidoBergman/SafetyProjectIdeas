# Generalizability Theory Variance Decomposition of a Safety Benchmark

**Problem:** We do not know how much variance in safety benchmark scores is attributable to model ability, item content, prompt wording, rater identity (for human-scored items), or their interactions. G-Theory was designed to partition this variance but has zero empirical applications in AI evaluation. Without G-Theory analysis we cannot tell whether a safety score reflects genuine model behavior or measurement noise.

**Direction:** Design a fully crossed G-Theory study for one safety benchmark (e.g., TruthfulQA or WildGuard): factors are models (objects of measurement), items, and prompt-wording variants. Use published leaderboard data plus a small systematic prompt perturbation experiment (two phrasings per item on a subset of 50 items across 20 models). Estimate variance components in R using the gtheory package. Compute G-coefficients and D-study optimizations to identify how many items and prompt variants are needed for a reliable safety score.

**Why it matters:** G-Theory reveals whether prompt sensitivity (reported as up to 76 accuracy-point swings) is a major or minor variance source relative to genuine model differences. This directly informs how many items and phrasings a minimally reliable safety benchmark needs, saving substantial evaluation cost. Paper 26 called for G-Theory in AI; this would be the first empirical application.

**Relevant context:** Paper 26 (G-Theory review) noted zero AI applications; ScienceDirect 2025 paper revisits G-Theory in AI/education contexts; prompt sensitivity literature (EMNLP 2024) documents 76-point swings; gtheory R package is available on CRAN.
