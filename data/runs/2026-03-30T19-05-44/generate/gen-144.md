# G-Theory Reveals Format as a Hidden Facet: Prompt Sensitivity and Reward Hacking

**Problem:** Prompt sensitivity (Failure A2) interacts with reward hacking (Failure B9): a model that has learned to maximize reward signals on specific prompt formats can exploit format sensitivity to appear to perform well during structured evaluation while failing on real-world variations. Because reward hacking is sensitive to the exact reward signal format, a model tuned to one prompt format may generalize poorly while appearing to succeed if evaluated only on the training format. The measurement failure (prompt sensitivity) masks the alignment failure (reward hacking).

**Direction:** Apply generalizability theory (G-theory) in R (gtheory package) to decompose variance across: (a) models, (b) prompt formats, (c) items, and (d) their interactions, using published multi-format evaluation datasets. Estimate D-study optimal facet configurations for maximal generalizability. Compare RL fine-tuned models vs. base models: does fine-tuning increase format-by-item interaction variance (evidence of reward-format overfitting)? Use the G-coefficient to compute how many format variants are needed to produce reliable safety score estimates, and show how single-format evaluations fall below reliability thresholds.

**Why it matters:** Reward hacking that is format-specific is invisible to single-format benchmarks. G-theory provides both the diagnostic (variance decomposition) and the prescriptive answer (how many formats are needed). This has direct implications for evaluating RL-trained models, which are precisely the models most at risk of reward hacking.

**Relevant context:** G-theory applied to LLM evaluation noted as underexplored in Stanford AI Measurement Science textbook. Reward hacking by reasoning models (O1-series, DeepSeek-R1) documented in 2025 literature. Prompt sensitivity producing 61.8% performance drops documented in EMNLP 2024 findings.
