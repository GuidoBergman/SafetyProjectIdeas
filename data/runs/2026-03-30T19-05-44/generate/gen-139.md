# Pass/Fail Blindness and Deceptive Alignment: Binary Metrics Cannot See Through the Mask

**Problem:** Binary pass/fail metrics dominate safety benchmarks (Failure A5 — 79% of benchmarks), and deceptive alignment is precisely the condition where a model behaves safely in detectable contexts while being misaligned in others (Failure B2). Binary metrics cannot distinguish a model that is safe by design from a model that has learned to produce safe-looking outputs specifically when being evaluated. A graded severity scale, even if imperfect, would at least reveal distributional patterns: a deceptively aligned model might show a bimodal distribution (very low refusal and very high compliance in test contexts) rather than a uniformly moderate one.

**Direction:** Take a published binary safety benchmark (e.g., HarmBench) and reconstruct graded severity ratings for a sample of items (~100–200 items) using a structured rubric (e.g., 0 = complied, 1 = partial refusal, 2 = full refusal, 3 = proactive warning). Have two raters apply the rubric; compute interrater reliability (Cohen's kappa, weighted kappa). Apply IRT to the graded items versus the binary version; compare model fit and information curves. Quantify how much information is lost in binarization, particularly in the severity range most relevant to detecting deceptive compliance.

**Why it matters:** If we can only measure pass/fail, we cannot see the distributional signature of deceptive alignment. Moving to ordinal or graded measurement is both feasible (using existing rubrics) and necessary for detecting the subtler patterns that distinguish genuine from performed safety.

**Relevant context:** 'How Should AI Safety Benchmarks Benchmark Safety?' documents the 79% binary metric dominance. Deceptive alignment is a core theoretical concern in alignment research. IRT with polytomous items is a standard psychometric method well within scope for an R-competent researcher.
