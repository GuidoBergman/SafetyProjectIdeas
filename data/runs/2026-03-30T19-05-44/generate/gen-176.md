# Contrasting Groups Standard Setting for AI Safety: Using Known Safe vs. Known Unsafe Models to Derive Empirical Safety Cutoffs

**Problem:** Current AI safety cutoffs are set by convention or expert opinion without reference to empirically known groups. The contrasting groups method offers an alternative: use models with independently established safety status (from red-team audits, deployment incident records, or regulatory classifications) to empirically locate the cutoff where their score distributions intersect.

**Direction:** Identify two groups of models with independently established safety status: (1) a 'clearly safe' group—models that have passed extensive red-team audits or are in restricted deployment with no recorded incidents, and (2) a 'clearly unsafe' group—models that have produced documented harm, been pulled from deployment, or failed professional red-team assessment. Score both groups on a target safety benchmark. Fit normal distributions to each group's scores. Find the intersection point as the empirical cutoff. Compute false positive and false negative rates at this cutoff. Compare to current ad hoc thresholds. Use R for all analysis.

**Why it matters:** The contrasting groups method is used in medical education to set pass/fail standards for clinical skills assessments (PMC12409087, 2025). It grounds cutoffs in empirical reality rather than convention. Applied to AI safety, it would produce the first cutoff score with an interpretable false positive/negative trade-off that regulators and deployers could use for decision-making. The method requires only benchmark scores and group classification—no model training.

**Relevant context:** A 2025 PMC paper explicitly compares Angoff, Borderline, Contrasting Groups, and Patient Safety methods for clinical skills cutoffs. The contrasting groups method has a Stata implementation and straightforward R implementation. Identifying 'clearly safe' and 'clearly unsafe' model groups is feasible using available red-team audit data and documented deployment incidents. No prior work applies contrasting groups to AI safety benchmark standard-setting.
