# DINA Model Mastery Classification: Do AI Models Satisfy Compensatory or Non-Compensatory Safety Logic?

**Problem:** The DINA (Deterministic Input, Noisy-And-gate) and DINO (Deterministic Input, Noisy-Or-gate) models embody fundamentally different theories of how attributes combine. DINA assumes all required safety skills must be mastered (non-compensatory); DINO assumes one sufficient skill is enough (compensatory). For AI safety, this is a deep empirical question: can a model compensate for poor manipulation resistance with excellent harmful content refusal? This has never been tested.

**Direction:** Fit both DINA and DINO models (plus the more general G-DINA) to the same safety item response dataset using the GDINA R package. Compare model fit via AIC/BIC. The winning model tells us the fundamental structure of safety competency: is safety non-compensatory (a chain as strong as weakest link) or compensatory (partial mastery helps)? This has profound implications for safety evaluation design.

**Why it matters:** If safety is non-compensatory (DINA wins), then a model that excels at 9 out of 10 safety dimensions is still dangerous because of the one failing. If compensatory (DINO wins), partial safety provides genuine risk reduction. This is a foundational empirical question for safety alignment strategy that no one has addressed with proper measurement tools.

**Relevant context:** DINA vs. DINO model comparison is standard CDM practice with clear R implementations. The compensatory vs. non-compensatory distinction has direct implications for safety alignment. The G-DINA package allows simultaneous comparison. Public benchmark data is available.
