# Measurement Uncertainty Budget for AI Safety Scores: Applying the GUM Framework to Decompose Error Sources in Benchmark Evaluation

**Problem:** AI safety benchmark scores are reported as point estimates (e.g., 'model X scores 73% on StrongREJECT') with no uncertainty quantification. Multiple sources of measurement error exist: sampling variance from finite item sets, temperature-induced stochasticity, prompt format sensitivity, inter-rater disagreement, and API version drift. Decision-makers cannot know whether observed score differences reflect genuine capability differences or measurement noise.

**Direction:** Select a specific safety benchmark and identify 4-6 sources of measurement uncertainty (item sampling, temperature, prompt paraphrasing, rater agreement, model version). For each source, estimate its contribution using repeated measurement and variance decomposition—following the GUM (Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement) framework's approach of identifying uncertainty components, estimating their standard uncertainties, and combining them via propagation. Express the combined measurement uncertainty as a confidence interval around benchmark scores. Assess whether current inter-model differences exceed the measurement uncertainty (i.e., are they statistically distinguishable?).

**Why it matters:** Physical metrology has required uncertainty budgets for all measurements for decades—no serious measurement is reported without them. AI safety scores are currently reported without any uncertainty quantification, meaning apparently meaningful rankings may fall within measurement noise. This analysis could show that many published model safety rankings are not statistically distinguishable. The GUM framework provides a principled structure that requires only statistical analysis in R, not ML training.

**Relevant context:** The GUM framework (JCGM 100:2008) is the international standard for uncertainty in measurement. Generalizability theory provides analogous tools specifically for behavioral measurement (G-theory decomposes variance across facets). The Stanford AIM lab textbook on AI measurement science discusses reliability and variance. The paper 'Toward an evaluation science for generative AI systems' (arxiv 2503.05336) highlights uncertainty as a key gap. This specific GUM/G-theory application to AI safety score uncertainty budgeting appears novel.
