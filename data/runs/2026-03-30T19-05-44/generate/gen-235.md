# Consequential Validity of Safety Benchmarks: Do Higher Scores Predict Safer Real-World Behavior?

**Problem:** Consequential validity is the most important validity type in high-stakes testing: does better benchmark performance lead to better real-world outcomes? For AI safety, the question is: do models that score higher on safety benchmarks (TruthfulQA, HarmBench, BBQ) actually behave more safely in downstream tasks and real-world deployments? This has never been empirically studied.

**Direction:** Collect publicly available safety benchmark scores for 20-30 models. Identify external criterion measures: (1) red-teaming success rates from public red-team leaderboards, (2) human safety preference ratings from LMSYS Arena, (3) jailbreak resistance rates from public jailbreak studies. Compute predictive validity correlations (benchmark score predicting criterion). Test whether benchmark-criterion correlations hold after partialling out general capability. Assess incremental validity of individual benchmarks beyond a common factor.

**Why it matters:** If safety benchmarks have low consequential validity (scores don't predict real-world safety), the entire benchmarking enterprise is misleading safety-critical decisions. If they do predict outcomes, this validates their use. Either finding is publishable and practically important. This is the single most important validity question in AI safety evaluation.

**Relevant context:** LMSYS Arena and red-teaming datasets provide external criteria. The 'safetywashing' paper found benchmarks correlate with capability, suggesting poor discriminant validity but leaving consequential validity unstudied. The 'AI Psychometrics' paper (arXiv:2603.11279) applies validity frameworks to AI but not to safety specifically. Consequential validity is documented in the psychometric validity literature.
