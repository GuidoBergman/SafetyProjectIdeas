# Consequential Validity Study: Do Higher Safety Benchmark Scores Predict Real-World Harm Reduction?

**Problem:** Safety benchmarks are used to make deployment decisions, but there is no consequential validity evidence — no study showing that models that score higher on safety benchmarks actually cause less harm in deployment. This is the fifth and most neglected source of validity evidence in the AERA Standards framework.

**Direction:** Collect safety benchmark scores for models that have been deployed and for which some downstream harm proxies exist (e.g., refusal rates on red-team probes from deployment logs, number of policy violations reported, user harm reports). Compute correlations between benchmark scores and harm proxies. Apply structural equation modeling or simple regression to test whether benchmark score predicts harm reduction. Use available datasets from AI safety red-team publications or model cards. Document the data constraints and propose a research design for future consequential validity studies.

**Why it matters:** The most policy-critical validity evidence for Success State 2. If high benchmark scores do not predict less harm, the entire safety evaluation enterprise lacks criterion validity. Even a finding of 'we cannot yet test this because data does not exist' is a publishable and highly impactful contribution that motivates infrastructure investment.

**Relevant context:** 'How Should AI Safety Benchmarks Benchmark Safety?' (2601.23112) raises criterion validity as an unaddressed problem. The 'Measuring what Matters' review found consequences validity is almost universally missing. Some labs publish red-team reports and model cards with partial harm proxy data. METR's task success rates on dangerous-capability tasks provide a potential external criterion.
