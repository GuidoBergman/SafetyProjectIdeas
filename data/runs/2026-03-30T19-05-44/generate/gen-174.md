# Generalizability Theory Analysis of AI Safety Evaluation: Decomposing Score Variance Across Items, Raters, Prompts, and Temperature

**Problem:** AI safety scores vary for many reasons beyond genuine model safety differences: item sampling, temperature stochasticity, evaluator (human or LLM judge) differences, prompt formulation, and run-to-run variation. It is unknown how much variance each source contributes, making it impossible to design more reliable evaluations or interpret score differences.

**Direction:** Design a crossed G-study for safety evaluation: measure several models on several items, scored by multiple raters (human + LLM judges), under multiple temperature settings, across multiple prompt variants. Use the gtheory R package to estimate variance components for each facet and their interactions. Construct the D-study to determine optimal numbers of items, raters, and temperature samples to achieve a target generalizability coefficient (e.g., G > 0.80). Compare G-coefficients for different safety benchmark designs. Report which variance source dominates and what evaluation design changes would most improve reliability.

**Why it matters:** G theory simultaneously models multiple error sources that classical reliability (Cronbach's alpha) cannot distinguish. The 'Toward an evaluation science for generative AI systems' paper (arxiv 2503.05336) identifies multi-source variance as a key gap in AI evaluation. The Stanford AIM lab textbook on AI measurement science discusses G-theory as applicable to AI evaluation. The gtheory R package makes G-study analysis straightforward. This application to AI safety evaluation appears to be genuinely novel.
