# Operational Construct Audit for 'Safety' in Frontier Model Evaluations

**Problem:** AI safety evaluations are built on implicit, under-specified constructs. The causal chain leading to deployment harm begins at the first link: no one has formally audited whether the word 'safety' is operationally defined consistently across major evaluation frameworks. Without a clear, agreed construct, every downstream measurement step (item selection, scoring, decision-making) inherits hidden definitional variance. This is the most upstream, most neglected link in the false-negative deployment chain.

**Direction:** Conduct a systematic content-validity audit of the five most-used AI safety evaluation frameworks (e.g., MLCommons AILuminate, AIR-Bench, TruthfulQA, HarmBench, METR threat-model evals). For each, extract the explicit or implicit construct definition of 'safety'. Code definitions using a taxonomy of dimensions (behavioral, probabilistic, consequentialist, deontological). Compute inter-rater agreement (Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha) on construct categorization among 2-3 independent raters. Produce a construct map showing definitional overlap and gaps. Identify dimensions that appear in policy documents (e.g., Seoul Summit) but are absent from benchmark coverage. Deliverable: a blog post + coded construct matrix, requiring only document analysis, qualitative coding, and standard agreement statistics in R.

**Why it matters:** If evaluators cannot agree on what safety means, no amount of psychometric sophistication at later stages can produce valid results. Documenting this gap forces the field to confront the foundational measurement problem before investing in technical fixes.

**Relevant context:** Ren et al. (2024/NeurIPS) identified that the field of AI safety remains poorly defined and inconsistently measured. The 'How Should AI Safety Benchmarks Benchmark Safety?' paper (arXiv 2601.23112) and the 'Measurement to Meaning' framework (arXiv 2505.10573) both call for validity-centered approaches. AIR 2024 taxonomy covers 314 risk categories; MLCommons covers 7. This divergence reflects construct disagreement rather than mere scope differences.
