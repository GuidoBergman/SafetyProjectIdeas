# Contaminated Compliance: Data Contamination Laundering Regulatory Benchmarks

**Problem:** Benchmark data contamination inflates scores (Failure A3), and regulators are increasingly relying on those scores for compliance determinations (Failure B6). These failures compound into a regulatory laundering loop: a model's training data contains benchmark items, inflating its reported safety score; that score is then used to satisfy an AI Act or similar compliance requirement; the compliance claim is invalid because the underlying evidence is contaminated. The model may be genuinely unsafe while appearing certified.

**Direction:** Select 3–5 safety benchmarks that are (a) publicly available and (b) referenced in regulatory frameworks or government reports (e.g., AIR-Bench, HELM Safety, MLCommons AI Safety Benchmark v0.5). For each, apply published contamination-detection methods (membership inference signals, exact match rates, n-gram overlap) available without model internals, using open-source models where weights are accessible. Document the contamination exposure rate. Pair with a reliability analysis: compute test-retest reliability differences between likely-contaminated and likely-clean items using coefficient alpha in R. Estimate how much score inflation transfers into a specific regulatory compliance threshold.

**Why it matters:** The EU AI Act and emerging national frameworks increasingly reference named benchmarks. If those benchmarks are contaminated, compliance determinations are built on meaningless evidence. This research makes that risk quantitative and regulatorily legible.

**Relevant context:** Survey papers on data contamination document widespread benchmark leakage in Mistral, Llama, Yi. MMLU exact-match guessing rates of 52–57% found for ChatGPT/GPT-4. Regulatory use of benchmarks documented in EU AI Watch 2025 and the International AI Safety Report.
