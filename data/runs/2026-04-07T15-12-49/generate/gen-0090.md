# Systematic Literature Review: What Percentage of AI Cyber Capability Claims Are Supported by Adequate Experimental Evidence?

**Problem:** Media, industry, and even academic literature makes claims about AI offensive capabilities (e.g., 'AI can hack any CTF challenge', 'AI poses existential risk to cybersecurity') that may not be supported by rigorous experimental evidence. No systematic review has assessed the evidentiary quality of published capability claims.

**Direction:** Identify 30-40 published claims about AI offensive cyber capability from 2024-2026 (academic papers, white papers, news articles). For each claim, assess: (1) experimental evidence quality (controlled study, anecdote, theoretical), (2) effect size reported, (3) replication status, (4) generalizability claims vs. actual study scope. Compute an evidence quality distribution across the literature.

**Why it matters:** Policy decisions about AI safety are based on the evidentiary landscape. If the majority of published capability claims are based on weak evidence, the field is building its safety frameworks on an unreliable foundation. A systematic review would reveal whether the current alarm is proportionate to the evidence.

**Relevant context:** SoK Potter et al. (arxiv 2504.05408) provided a systematic overview but focused on findings not evidentiary quality. Benchmarking Practices paper (arxiv 2504.10112) identified methodological weaknesses. Systematic review methodology is standard in medical and social science research. This would be the first evidentiary quality audit of the AI cyber capability literature.
