# Do SAE Features for Harmful Intent Generalize Out-of-Distribution?

**Problem:** DeepMind's safety team found in 2025 that SAE-based detectors for harmful intent underperformed simple linear probes on out-of-distribution (OOD) test sets, even when proxy metrics like reconstruction loss and sparsity looked strong. The specific failure mode is that harmful-intent features learned from one distribution of prompts (e.g., direct requests) do not transfer to different phrasings (e.g., roleplay framing or indirect requests).

**Direction:** Using TransformerLens and a small open model (e.g. Gemma-2-2b), train a small SAE on activations from a curated dataset of direct harmful-intent prompts (from publicly available red-teaming datasets). Identify the top 10 features with highest mean activation on harmful vs. benign prompts. Then test these same features on 3 OOD reframings: (1) roleplay-framed requests, (2) third-person hypothetical framings, (3) indirect requests. Compare per-class AUROC of SAE features vs. a logistic regression probe on the same activations. Quantify the OOD generalization gap.

**Why it matters:** If interpretability-based safety detectors only work on the distribution they were found on, they provide false assurance. This experiment would concretely characterize the OOD failure boundary and help researchers understand whether the failure is about features not generalizing or SAE dictionaries not capturing the right decomposition.

**Relevant context:** DeepMind's 2025 blog post explicitly documented SAE underperformance vs. linear probes on OOD safety tasks. This is a documented, reproducible failure mode that merits systematic boundary-condition testing.
