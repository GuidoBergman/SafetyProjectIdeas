# Testing Whether Linear Probes Deceive: Can a Model Satisfy a Probe While Behaving Inconsistently?

**Problem:** Linear probes are widely used to claim that a model 'represents' a concept (e.g., truth, danger, sentiment). For safety, we need to know whether high probe accuracy implies that the concept is causally represented in a way that controls behavior, or whether models can satisfy probes while using a different internal representation for behavioral decisions.

**Direction:** On GPT-2 Small, train a linear probe for a concept (e.g., factual vs. non-factual statements) on layer 6 activations and achieve >90% probe accuracy. Then perform causal interventions: patch in the activation directions corresponding to 'factual' on prompts where the model would complete non-factually, and vice versa. Measure whether behavioral outputs change as the probe predicts. Test on 100 examples and report the causal effect size.

**Why it matters:** Backcasting from a world where interpretability findings about model representations reliably predict and control model behavior: this tests whether probes are causally meaningful. If probe-identified representations do not control behavior, probe-based safety claims are unfounded, which is a critical finding for the field.

**Relevant context:** The linear representation hypothesis has been partially falsified by 2025 research showing nonlinear 'onion' representations in small networks. Neel Nanda's 2025 update on interpretability pessimism connects to this concern. TransformerLens activation patching is the standard causal intervention tool.
