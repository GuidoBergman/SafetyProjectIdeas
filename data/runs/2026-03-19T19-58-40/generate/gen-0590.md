# Do adversarial suffixes (GCG) work by pushing refusal tokens to later positions or by suppressing them entirely?

**Problem:** Qi et al. argue that GCG adversarial suffixes exploit shallow alignment by causing the model to generate a non-refusal first token. But whether GCG works by delaying refusal (shifting it to position 3-5) vs. eliminating it across all positions is not established.

**Direction:** Run GCG attacks on a small aligned model on StrongReject prompts. For each successful jailbreak, log the full output token distribution at every position and measure: (a) the position at which refusal probability first drops below 5%; (b) whether refusal probability ever recovers after the first token. Compare this profile to failed attacks (non-jailbreaks). Also compare to prefilling attacks as a baseline.

**Why it matters:** If GCG merely delays refusal, defenses that check intermediate tokens (rather than just position 1) could catch it. If it suppresses globally, the mechanism is more severe. This shapes the design of both detection and alignment-training methods.

**Relevant context:** GCG (Zou et al. 2023, arXiv:2307.15043) is the canonical white-box suffix attack. Qi et al. link its success to shallow alignment. Recent Mask-GCG work (arXiv:2509.06350) analyses token importance in suffixes. Token-level probability logging is straightforward with any HuggingFace model.
