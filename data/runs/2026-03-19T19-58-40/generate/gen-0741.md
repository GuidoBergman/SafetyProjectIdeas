# Activation Clustering False Assurance: Benign and Harmful Prompts Share Residual Stream Neighborhoods

**Problem:** AI control systems sometimes use clustering of residual stream activations to flag anomalous (potentially harmful) inputs. If harmful prompts are not anomalous in activation space — because they resemble benign prompts until the final few tokens — this clustering-based monitoring fails silently. Failure mode 1: activations do not cleanly separate by harm type at monitored layers. Failure mode 2: the anomaly detector has no signal to work with, providing false assurance.

**Direction:** On a small model, collect residual stream activations for a balanced set of harmful and benign prompts at multiple layers. Apply UMAP and k-means clustering to visualize and quantify the separability of harmful vs. benign prompts in activation space. Measure at which layer (if any) separation becomes detectable. Test whether this threshold shifts for adversarially constructed prompts designed to look benign until the last token.

**Why it matters:** Clustering-based anomaly detection is a core component of many AI control proposals. If harmful prompts are not anomalous in activation space, this entire monitoring paradigm needs rethinking before being deployed in safety-critical systems.

**Relevant context:** The Oxford AIGI automated interpretability agenda describes population-level activation monitoring. Goldowsky-Dill's deception probe work notes aggregate unlabeled activations provide population-level structure — but this may not generalize to harm detection.
