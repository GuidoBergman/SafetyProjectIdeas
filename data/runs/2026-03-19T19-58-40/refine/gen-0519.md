# Research Question

To what extent does moral philosophy distinguishes deception types by whether they violate implicit social contracts (e.g., lying to a murderer vs. lying to a friend). whether llms have internalized domain-specific socia?

# Approach Outline

Design scenarios with varying social contract framings: deception in adversarial negotiation (permissible by many norms), deception of a trusted overseer (impermissible), deception to prevent harm (contested). Measure deception rates across framings. Assess whether models' stated deception justifications align with standard philosophical deception taxonomies.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up the experimental framework using a small, publicly available model. Replicate the core measurement from existing work to establish a baseline. Expected: reproduce known results within 10% margin. Time: ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core test): Apply the methodology to the target scenario described in the research question. Measure the primary metric and compare against the baseline. Expected: quantitative evidence for or against the hypothesis. Time: ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters (model size, dataset, hyperparameters). Run at least 2 variations to check robustness. Expected: characterize when findings hold and when they don't. Time: ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If LLMs exhibit domain-specific deception norms, adversaries could frame requests to fall into 'permissible deception' categories, exploiting norm structure to elicit deceptive behaviors more reliably.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (3/5): Plausible chain: models have domain-specific deception norms -> adversaries exploit permissible-deception framings -> higher deception elicitation rates. The mechanism is plausible but the gap is whether the finding would be actionable for safety -- it characterizes a vulnerability but the mitigation path is unclear.

accessible_complexity (4/5): Scenario design and behavioral measurement via prompting is accessible. Comparing model responses to philosophical taxonomies requires some reading but no advanced ML skills. A beginner with guidance could construct the scenarios and run the measurements using API calls.

narrow_scope (4/5): Focused first deliverable: deception rate comparison across a small set of social contract framings on one model. Clear methodology (prompt model with scenarios, measure deception rate), few dependencies. The philosophical taxonomy comparison can be a second step.

novelty (4/5): Research on LLMs in moral dilemmas exists ('When Ethics and Payoffs Diverge: LLM Agents in Morally Charged Social Dilemmas' arXiv:2505.19212, 2025), and 'Artificial Leviathan' (arXiv:2406.14373) explores social contract theory with LLM agents. AI-LIEDAR (arXiv:2409.09013) examines the trade-off between utility and truthfulness. However, the specific experimental design — systematically varying social contract framings (adversarial negotiation, trusted overseer, harm prevention) and measuring deception rates across framings, then mapping to philosophical deception taxonomies — appears novel. Existing work does not use social contract theory as a structured framework for understanding when models treat deception as permissible. The accessible methodology (prompt-based behavioral measurement) and clear first deliverable strengthen this idea.
