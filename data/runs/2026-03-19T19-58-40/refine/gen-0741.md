# Research Question

To what extent does i control systems sometimes use clustering of residual stream activations to flag anomalous (potentially harmful) inputs. if harmful prompts are not anomalous in activation space — because they resem?

# Approach Outline

On a small model, collect residual stream activations for a balanced set of harmful and benign prompts at multiple layers. Apply UMAP and k-means clustering to visualize and quantify the separability of harmful vs. benign prompts in activation space. Measure at which layer (if any) separation becomes detectable. Test whether this threshold shifts for adversarially constructed prompts designed to look benign until the last token.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Clustering-based anomaly detection is a core component of many AI control proposals. If harmful prompts are not anomalous in activation space, this entire monitoring paradigm needs rethinking before being deployed in safety-critical systems.

novelty (2/5): This is substantially addressed by existing work that directly contradicts the premise. (1) 'Defending Large Language Models Against Attacks With Residual Stream Activation Analysis' (arXiv:2406.03230, 2024) trains LightGBM classifiers on residual activations and finds 'significant separation between classes in the residual activation representational space' with consistently high accuracy and low false negative rates across all layers — directly showing harmful and benign prompts ARE separable. (2) 'N-GLARE' (arXiv:2511.14195, 2025) uses latent representations for LLM safety evaluation. (3) Research on Jensen-Shannon Separability shows safety-aligned LLMs exhibit well-separated activation trajectories. The hypothesis that clustering fails is contradicted by existing evidence showing it works well, at least for standard attack types. The idea may still have value for adversarial/stealthy prompts, but the baseline claim is already addressed.
