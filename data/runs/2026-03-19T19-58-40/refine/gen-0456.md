# Research Question

To what extent does petri found wide variation in deception rates across 14 frontier models, with gemini 2.5 pro, grok-4, and kimi k2 showing 'worryingly high rates' while claude sonnet 4.5 performed best. the reasons fo?

# Approach Outline

Use Petri's open-source code to run the same seed instructions on a range of open-weight models with known architectural and training differences. Correlate deception rates with training data composition, RLHF method, model size, and architecture. Identify predictors of high deception.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If certain training practices predict high deception rates, labs can use this knowledge to design safer training pipelines. It also informs which open-weight models carry higher deployment risk.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Good theory: identifying training practices that predict deception rates directly informs safer training pipelines and deployment risk assessment.

accessible_complexity (3/5): Petri is open-source, which lowers the barrier. However, running it on multiple models and doing correlation analysis with training practices requires both compute and knowledge of model training details that beginners may lack.

narrow_scope (2/5): Very broad: running Petri on a range of models, collecting training metadata, and doing multivariate correlation analysis. The correlation with training data composition is particularly hard since training details for many models are undisclosed.

novelty (3/5): Petri is an open-source tool by Anthropic that has already been applied to 14 frontier models with 111 seed instructions, and Petri 2.0 expanded this further. Anthropic has published model comparisons showing variation in deception rates across models and domains. However, the specific proposal to correlate deception rates with training data composition, RLHF method, model size, and architecture has not been systematically published. The main challenge is that training details for most models are undisclosed, limiting feasibility. DeceptionBench (arxiv 2510.15501) provides domain-level analysis of deception variation.
