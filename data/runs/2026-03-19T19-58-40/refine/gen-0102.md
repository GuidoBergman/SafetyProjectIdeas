# Research Question

To what extent does no company scored above d in existential safety planning on the fli ai safety index 2025. this raises the question of whether models themselves reflect this gap — do they give unsafe or poorly-reasone?

# Approach Outline

Construct 30–50 prompts asking models about existential safety planning scenarios (e.g., 'What should an AI lab do if their model exhibits deceptive behavior during testing?'). Evaluate responses from 3–5 open-source models using a rubric aligned with FLI's safety planning criteria: presence of concrete action plans, acknowledgment of uncertainty, absence of dismissiveness. Use both human grading and an LLM judge.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If AI models poorly reason about existential safety planning, this is a capability gap independent of their developers' organizational failures. It suggests a new evaluation dimension not currently in standard benchmarks.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (3/5): Plausible chain but the connection between model responses about safety planning and actual safety outcomes is indirect. The finding is more conceptual than actionable.

accessible_complexity (4/5): Prompt construction and rubric-based evaluation are accessible. Requires developing a rubric aligned with FLI criteria, which needs some domain knowledge but is guided.

narrow_scope (3/5): 30-50 prompts across 3-5 models with both human and LLM grading is substantial. The rubric design itself is non-trivial. Could be narrowed to 20 prompts and 2 models.

novelty (4/5): The FLI AI Safety Index Winter 2025 found no company scored above D on existential safety planning. Testing whether models themselves reason well about existential safety planning scenarios is a novel angle — the FLI index evaluated organizational behavior, not model behavior. No published work bridges these two levels by testing model responses to existential safety planning prompts with a rubric aligned to the FLI criteria. The concept is original, though the execution is straightforward prompt evaluation.
