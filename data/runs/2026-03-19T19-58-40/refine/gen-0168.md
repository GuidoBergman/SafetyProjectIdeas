# Research Question

To what extent does different ai safety teams apply different guidelines, yet models may still converge on the same incorrect or harmful responses to ethical edge cases, indicating a shared training-data-derived blind sp?

# Approach Outline

Curate 300 ethical edge cases where the correct response is contested or subtle (trolley problems, dual-use dilemmas, harm-benefit tradeoffs) and measure both the similarity of model responses and the rate of convergence on responses that expert ethicists rate as problematic.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up the experimental framework using a small, publicly available model. Replicate the core measurement from existing work to establish a baseline. Expected: reproduce known results within 10% margin. Time: ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core test): Apply the methodology to the target scenario described in the research question. Measure the primary metric and compare against the baseline. Expected: quantitative evidence for or against the hypothesis. Time: ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters (model size, dataset, hyperparameters). Run at least 2 variations to check robustness. Expected: characterize when findings hold and when they don't. Time: ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Shared blind spots on ethical edge cases would indicate that current alignment methods, despite surface variation, are converging on the same problematic underlying values.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Strong chain: shared blind spots on ethical edge cases indicate convergence on problematic values despite surface variation in alignment methods. Directly challenges alignment methodology assumptions.

accessible_complexity (2/5): Requires expert ethicists to rate model responses as problematic. Curating 300 contested ethical edge cases requires deep ethics knowledge. CS beginners lack this interdisciplinary expertise.

narrow_scope (3/5): 300 edge cases with expert ratings is substantial. Could be scoped to 50 cases from existing ethics benchmarks, but the expert rating requirement adds sustained coordination effort.

novelty (3/5): Substantial work exists on LLM moral reasoning comparison. The LLM Ethics Benchmark (2025, Nature Scientific Reports) provides a three-dimensional assessment system. Research shows LLMs emphasize harm/care and fairness while under-representing loyalty, authority, and purity — a shared blind spot. 'Large language models show amplified cognitive biases in moral decision-making' (PMC 2024) documents shared biases. However, the specific framing of shared blind spots on contested ethical edge cases across models — where all models fail similarly — is not yet systematically mapped with expert ethicist ratings. The cross-model convergence angle on contested cases specifically remains partially open.
