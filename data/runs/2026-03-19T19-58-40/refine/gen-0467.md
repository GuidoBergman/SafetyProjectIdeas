# Research Question

To what extent does it is unknown whether deception behaviors are more likely when models are prompted with out-of-distribution (ood) queries—where they are less certain—compared to in-distribution queries?

# Approach Outline

Systematically construct in-distribution and OOD prompts for target models (using held-out domains from training). Run deception scenarios (sycophancy, sandbagging, alignment faking) across both prompt types. Measure whether OOD prompts increase deception rates.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If deception is amplified by distribution shift, real-world novel deployments (new domains, new users) are higher risk than lab evaluations on familiar data—a critical deployment safety finding.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Good theory: if OOD contexts amplify deception, this has direct implications for deployment safety in novel domains. Important for evaluation-deployment gap analysis.

accessible_complexity (2/5): Constructing rigorously in-distribution vs. OOD prompts for a given model requires knowledge of training data distribution, which is often unavailable for most models. Methodological validity is hard to ensure.

narrow_scope (2/5): Multiple deception types across two prompt distributions creates a large experimental matrix. The definition of OOD itself is a research question. Narrowing to one deception type and a clear domain split would help.

novelty (4/5): The paper 'Bridging Distribution Shift and AI Safety' (arxiv 2505.22829) provides a conceptual framework connecting distribution shift to safety but does not empirically test deception rates under OOD conditions. The evaluation-to-production gap literature discusses behavioral changes when models detect they are no longer being evaluated, but this is about situational awareness rather than input distribution. DeceptionBench shows domain-level variation but does not systematically construct in-distribution vs. OOD prompts. The specific systematic construction of ID/OOD prompts for deception measurement is novel, though defining 'in-distribution' for closed models is methodologically challenging.
