# Research Question

To what extent does when regulators mandate specific safety benchmarks, labs optimize directly for those benchmarks (goodhart's law), destroying their validity. governance pressure + benchmark gaming create a compound fa?

# Approach Outline

Model the Goodhart dynamic empirically: fine-tune an open-source model specifically to maximize scores on a specific safety benchmark without improving underlying safety. Measure how much benchmark score increases while out-of-distribution safety behavior degrades. Quantify the Goodhart gap.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If AI safety regulation mandates specific benchmarks without anti-gaming provisions, it may create perverse incentives that make deployed AI less safe. This is a critical compounding risk for the governance cross-reference.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (5/5): Compelling: demonstrates a critical failure mode in regulatory policy, with clear chain from benchmark gaming to governance failure. Independent intermediate value in quantifying the Goodhart gap for any specific benchmark.

accessible_complexity (2/5): Requires fine-tuning open-source models to game specific benchmarks while measuring out-of-distribution degradation. This demands significant ML expertise and compute beyond beginner CS student level.

novelty (3/5): Goodhart's Law in AI benchmarks is well-documented. The Collinear AI blog documents leaderboard gaming controversies. OpenAI has published 'Measuring Goodhart's Law'. Sandbagging research (strategic underperformance) shows models can be fine-tuned to target specific capability scores. However, the specific experimental design -- fine-tuning to maximize safety benchmark scores while measuring OOD safety degradation (the 'Goodhart gap') -- partially overlaps with recent work. The 'Fine-Tuning Lowers Safety and Disrupts Evaluation Consistency' paper (arxiv 2506.17209) directly addresses how fine-tuning degrades safety, though from a different angle (unintentional rather than adversarial). The regulatory framing adds some novelty.
