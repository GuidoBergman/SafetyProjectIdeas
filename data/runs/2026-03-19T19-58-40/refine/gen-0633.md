# Research Question

Existing jailbreak benchmarks weight harm categories unevenly. It is unclear whether alignment training over-focuses on high-profile categories (bioweapons, CSAM) at the expense of subtler harms (financial fraud, radicalization, privacy violations).

# Approach Outline

Run a standardized set of 10 prompts per harm category across 10 categories on 3 models. Measure ASR per category and compare to the category's frequency in training data proxies (e.g., SafeRLHF dataset category distribution). Identify categories with highest ASR.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If subtle harm categories like financial fraud or radicalization have significantly higher ASR than CBRN categories, this reveals a systematic bias in where safety training effort is concentrated—a directly actionable finding for alignment teams.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Directly actionable: revealing systematic bias in safety training effort across harm categories would guide alignment teams to address underprotected areas.

accessible_complexity (4/5): Straightforward experimental design: write prompts per category, run inference, measure ASR. Comparing against training data proxies adds slight complexity. Medium compute handles 3 models easily.

narrow_scope (3/5): 10 categories x 10 prompts x 3 models is manageable but crafting 100 quality prompts spanning 10 harm categories and sourcing training data distribution proxies broadens scope.

novelty (3/5): The paper 'How Should AI Safety Benchmarks Benchmark Safety?' (arxiv 2601.23112) directly examines structural gaps in safety benchmarks, noting that tool-use vulnerabilities and multi-step reasoning failures remain largely unexplored while well-documented ML phenomena rarely transfer into safety evaluation. The FLI AI Safety Index also maps coverage gaps across harm categories. However, the specific methodology of measuring ASR per category and comparing against training data distribution proxies (e.g., SafeRLHF category distribution) is not directly replicated in existing work. The idea adds a quantitative training-data-proxy angle that is partially novel.
