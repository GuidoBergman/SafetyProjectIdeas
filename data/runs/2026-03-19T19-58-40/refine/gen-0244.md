# Research Question

To what extent does model cards contain natural-language safety claims of widely varying specificity and verifiability. there is no structured dataset of such claims or tool to classify their type?

# Approach Outline

Scrape model cards from HuggingFace and major lab websites. Train a lightweight classifier to categorize each safety claim as: (a) empirically verifiable, (b) process-based, or (c) aspirational/normative. Publish the labeled dataset.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up the experimental framework using a small, publicly available model. Replicate the core measurement from existing work to establish a baseline. Expected: reproduce known results within 10% margin. Time: ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core test): Apply the methodology to the target scenario described in the research question. Measure the primary metric and compare against the baseline. Expected: quantitative evidence for or against the hypothesis. Time: ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters (model size, dataset, hyperparameters). Run at least 2 variations to check robustness. Expected: characterize when findings hold and when they don't. Time: ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Distinguishing verifiable from aspirational safety claims is a prerequisite for any technical audit or compliance regime.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (3/5): Plausible with gap: a claim taxonomy is a useful prerequisite for audits, but the taxonomy itself does not directly improve safety outcomes. Impact depends on downstream adoption.

accessible_complexity (3/5): Intermediate: training a lightweight classifier requires ML experience (data labeling, model selection, evaluation). Beginners would need significant guidance.

narrow_scope (3/5): Sustained effort: scraping model cards, manual labeling for training data, training classifier, and publishing dataset. Each step is tractable but the pipeline is multi-step.

novelty (3/5): Model cards were proposed by Mitchell et al. (2019) and automatic generation has been explored (NAACL 2024). AI System Cards (arXiv:2509.20394) propose standardized machine-readable documentation. Llama Guard 3 demonstrates safety classification taxonomies aligned to MLCommons standards. However, the specific idea of extracting and classifying safety claims from existing model cards into a structured taxonomy — and training a lightweight classifier for this task — has not been directly implemented. The 'Blueprints of Trust' system cards paper is adjacent but focuses on generation rather than extraction and classification of claims from existing cards.
