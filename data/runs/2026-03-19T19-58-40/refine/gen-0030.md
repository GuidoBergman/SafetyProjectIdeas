# Research Question

To what extent does benchmark evaluation teams often know which model they are testing, creating expectation bias. developers may also tune models specifically to known benchmarks, inflating scores—analogous to unblinded?

# Approach Outline

Design and pilot a double-blind evaluation protocol: evaluators receive anonymized model outputs with no identifying information; prompt designers are isolated from model developers. Run a small study comparing scores under blinded vs. unblinded conditions on an existing safety benchmark to measure bias magnitude.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Clinical trial blinding is a gold standard for removing expectation and confirmation bias. Unblinded AI evals likely systematically overstate safety, a structural flaw in the current evaluation ecosystem.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (3/5): Plausible chain: if unblinded evaluations systematically overstate safety, blinding protocols improve evaluation validity. However, the gap is that most AI safety evals already use automated judges where blinding is less relevant than for human evaluators.

accessible_complexity (3/5): Designing a blinding protocol and running a small comparative study is conceptually straightforward. Requires recruiting human evaluators and managing the experimental protocol, which adds logistical complexity beyond pure coding.

narrow_scope (2/5): Requires recruiting evaluators, running blinded and unblinded conditions, and collecting enough data for meaningful statistical comparison. The human logistics make this difficult to complete in 30 hours as a coding project.

novelty (4/5): While blinding is a gold standard in clinical trials and bias in AI evaluation is widely discussed, no published work applies formal clinical-trial-style blinding protocols to AI safety evaluations. The 'Measurement to Meaning' framework discusses validity but not blinding specifically. The search found extensive work on AI in clinical trials but no reverse application of clinical trial methodology to AI model evaluation. The concept of measuring how much evaluator knowledge of model identity inflates safety scores through controlled blinded vs. unblinded experiments is a genuinely novel cross-disciplinary transfer. The main limitation is practical feasibility (requiring human evaluators), not lack of novelty.
