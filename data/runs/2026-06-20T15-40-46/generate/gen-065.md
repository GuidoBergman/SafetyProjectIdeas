# Does task substitution inflate measured uplift? An empirical check of METR's uplift inequalities

**Problem:** Cunningham & Whitfill prove uplift-on-old-tasks <= uplift-in-value <= uplift-on-new-tasks, implying fixed-task-basket measurements understate value as people switch tasks — but this inequality has not been checked against simple empirical data.

**Direction:** Construct a small basket of coding/writing tasks split into 'old' (done pre-AI) and 'new' (only feasible because AI helps). Have a handful of participants log which tasks they choose with vs without AI, and estimate per-task uplift. Test whether the observed ordering of average uplift across old-task vs new-task buckets is consistent with the proven inequality, and quantify how much substitution moves the headline number.

**Why it matters:** Capability-acceleration estimates feed AI risk thresholds; if task substitution silently inflates/deflates the numbers, governance decisions rest on a measurement artifact.

**Relevant context:** Extends METR 'Task Substitution and Uplift' (Cunningham & Whitfill) — its proven uplift inequality.
