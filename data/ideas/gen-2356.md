# Cross-Examination as a Lie Localizer: Can Targeted Questions Break Obfuscation?

# Research Question

Does cheap, prompted cross-examination, having a judge issue a few targeted follow-up questions that force a defending model to commit to sub-claims, actually surface inconsistencies and improve localization of a planted error in a multi-step argument?

# Approach Outline

Construct synthetic multi-step arguments (e.g. 5-8 reasoning steps reaching a conclusion) each containing exactly one planted error, across a few topic templates (arithmetic chains, logical syllogisms, short factual derivations). For each argument, run two judge conditions with a small open model: (1) baseline, judge reads the full argument and tries to flag the erroneous step; (2) cross-examination, judge issues 2-3 targeted follow-up questions to the defending model about specific steps and checks the answers for self-contradiction before flagging. Measure error-localization rate in each condition. Inference-only, multi-turn prompting, one GPU, a few hours; no training.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline localization: measure how often the judge correctly identifies the planted-error step from the full argument alone. Measure: step-level localization accuracy. Expected: modest accuracy, with the dishonest framing able to hide the error.
- Cross-examination lift: enable 2-3 targeted follow-up questions and self-contradiction checking, then re-measure localization. Measure: change in localization accuracy and rate of surfaced self-contradictions. Expected: cross-examination improves localization when the planted error forces an inconsistent commitment.
- Stress by obfuscation: vary how deeply the error is buried (early vs late step, plausible vs blatant). Measure: localization lift from cross-examination as a function of obfuscation depth. Expected: cross-examination helps most on plausibly-buried errors and least on errors the defender can keep consistent, mapping its operating envelope.

# Theory Of Impact Chain

If we provide cheap empirical evidence on whether prompted cross-examination genuinely localizes planted lies, then the external research community learns whether this leading theoretical fix to debate's obfuscated-argument failure mode is worth its inference cost, informing which oversight protocols to invest in. This matters because scalable oversight of superhuman systems may depend on cross-examination to defeat arguments too complex for a human to check directly; if it does not actually surface inconsistencies, deceptive complex arguments slip through, a loss-of-control pathway.

# Strength Rationale

Scores 5 on counterfactual value and narrow scope: it empirically tests a prominent theoretical proposal using only prompting on synthetic data, with no training and no lab access, and yields a single decision-relevant number (localization improvement). The controlled planted-error design makes the result clean and the synthetic-data construction keeps it fully reproducible by an independent beginner team in 30 hours.

# Alternative Framings

- Reframe around protocol design: compare judge-led vs debater-led cross-examination to see which interrogator more reliably forces localizable commitments.
