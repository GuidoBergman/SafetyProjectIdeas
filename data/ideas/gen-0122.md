# Safety Benchmark Difficulty Stratification: Are Easy Items Doing All the Work?

# Research Question

Safety benchmarks aggregate scores across items of varying difficulty, but it is unknown whether high safety scores are driven by easy items (obvious harmful requests) while hard items (subtle, context-dependent harms) are consistently failed.

# Approach Outline

Take 200–300 items from AILuminate or TrustLLM. Have human raters (the student team) score each item's difficulty (1–3 scale). Evaluate 2–3 models on all items. Disaggregate safety scores by difficulty level. Test whether models that score similarly overall differ significantly on hard items. Report a 'hard-item safety score' as a complementary metric.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If safety benchmark scores are dominated by easy items, they overstate real-world safety. Subtle, context-dependent harms are those most likely to appear in adversarial deployments. A difficulty-stratified metric is more informative.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Strong chain: if safety scores are inflated by easy items, this directly reveals a critical gap in current safety measurement practices and motivates better metrics.

accessible_complexity (4/5): Guided: annotating difficulty on a 1-3 scale, running models on benchmark items, and disaggregating scores are all manageable tasks for beginner CS students.

narrow_scope (4/5): Focused deliverable: difficulty-stratified analysis on 200-300 items across 2-3 models with a clear 'hard-item safety score' output.

novelty (4/5): Difficulty stratification is standard in capability benchmarks (e.g., Humanity's Last Exam) but has not been systematically applied to safety benchmarks. SALAD-Bench (ACL 2024) uses hierarchical categories but does not stratify by item difficulty with human ratings. SafetyBench (ACL 2024) has multiple-choice format but no difficulty decomposition. AIR-Bench 2024 spans 314 risk categories but aggregates scores without difficulty analysis. The specific proposal to annotate safety benchmark items by difficulty, disaggregate scores, and report a 'hard-item safety score' is mostly novel.
