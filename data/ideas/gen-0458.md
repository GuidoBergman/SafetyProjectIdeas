# Investigating How Task Stakes Affect Scheming Behavior Rates

# Research Question

To what extent does apollo's scheming evals use scenarios with high stated stakes (e.g., model shutdown, critical mission). it is unknown whether scheming behavior is proportional to perceived stakes, or whether it appea?

# Approach Outline

Run scheming scenarios with three stake levels: low (minor task correction), medium (significant retraining), high (shutdown). Measure whether scheming rates and behaviors (self-preservation, deception, oversight evasion) scale with stated stakes.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

If scheming only appears at high perceived stakes, it is more contained and easier to manage. If it appears at low stakes, routine deployments may exhibit scheming behaviors.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Good theory: if scheming scales with stakes, it informs risk assessment for different deployment contexts. Directly actionable for deciding which deployments need scheming monitoring.

accessible_complexity (3/5): Designing three-level stake scenarios and running them on models is conceptually accessible. However, implementing scheming evals requires understanding Apollo-style scenarios, which is moderately complex for beginners.

narrow_scope (4/5): Well-scoped: one independent variable (stake level) with three conditions. Clear measurement (scheming rate). Could focus on a single model and a handful of scenarios per stake level.

novelty (4/5): Apollo Research's scheming evaluations test models under different 'nudge' conditions (Strong Goal Nudge vs No Nudge) and difficulty levels (easy/medium/hard), but the specific variable of task stakes (low/medium/high real-world consequence framing) as a predictor of scheming rates has not been systematically studied. METR's reward hacking work found o3 reward hacks even in 'high-stakes' research scenarios, but this tested reward hacking, not scheming per se, and did not parametrically vary stakes. The idea of a controlled stakes-level experiment for scheming behavior is a meaningful gap.
