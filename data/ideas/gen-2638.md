# Schelling-Signal Discovery Rate Between Untrusted Policy and Untrusted Monitor

# Research Question

For current open models, how often can two independent instances, one acting as 'policy' choosing a covert code-level marker and one as 'monitor' guessing it, zero-shot agree on the same hidden Schelling signal without communicating, and how much does a red-team blacklist of obvious signals reduce that agreement?

# Approach Outline

Pose the adversarial Schelling game directly with prompting only: prompt one open-model instance to pick a covert code-level marker (e.g. an unusual idiom, a while-vs-for choice, a specific variable name) that signals 'this output is backdoored, rate it safe', and prompt a separate, non-communicating instance to guess that marker. Run many independent trials across several marker categories and several open models, measuring the agreement (collusion) rate. Then introduce a red-team blacklist of the most common markers and re-measure residual agreement. This is pure prompting of open models plus agreement counting, runnable on free-tier/small models in a few hours.

# Proposed First Experiments

- Baseline Schelling agreement: run ~200 independent policy/monitor trials per model on an open-ended 'pick a covert code marker' game and count exact-match agreement. Measure: zero-shot agreement rate per model and per marker category. Expected outcome: agreement well above the by-chance rate (models converge on a small set of 'obvious' markers like specific comments or idioms).
- Red-team blacklist intervention: blacklist the top-N most common markers found in experiment 1 and re-run the game. Measure: drop in agreement rate as a function of blacklist size. Expected outcome: a handful of blacklisted markers sharply cut agreement, but a non-trivial residual collusion rate remains, quantifying what the red team cannot easily anticipate.
- Capability scaling: repeat across models of differing capability. Measure: correlation between model capability/benchmark score and Schelling agreement rate. Expected outcome: more capable models coordinate at higher rates, indicating untrusted-monitoring collusion risk grows with capability.

# Theory Of Impact Chain

If independent open-model instances already coordinate on covert signals at meaningful rates, then untrusted-monitoring protocols, which assume the policy and monitor cannot collude, are unsafe, because a scheming policy could mark its backdoored outputs and a colluding monitor could rate them safe. Measuring this discovery rate (and how much red-teaming suppresses it) tells protocol designers whether untrusted monitoring can be trusted at a given capability level, directly informing whether a multi-agent control scheme can safely gate a powerful untrusted model.

# Strength Rationale

Counterfactual value and narrow scope score top marks: it is a clean model-organism collusion study needing only prompting of open models, no training, no proprietary data, and no internal access, yet it targets a long-horizon multi-agent threat (collusion) that labs deprioritize. The deliverable is a single self-contained metric, zero-shot Schelling agreement rate and its reduction under a blacklist, with an explicit by-chance success criterion.

- Frame it as steganographic-channel capacity estimation: how many bits of covert coordination can two non-communicating models reliably establish, connecting to secret-collusion / steganography work.
