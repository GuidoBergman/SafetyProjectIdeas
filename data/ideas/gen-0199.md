# Homogeneity in Refusal Reasoning: Do Models Cite the Same Reasons for Refusing the Same Requests?

# Research Question

To what extent does even when models produce different surface-level refusals, they may cite the same underlying reasons (e.g., always citing 'potential for harm' over other legitimate considerations), indicating deeper ?

# Approach Outline

Extract and categorize the stated reasons for refusals from 15+ models across 500 harmful-prompt scenarios. Measure diversity of refusal reasoning types (harm-based, policy-based, uncertainty-based, contextual) and compute inter-model similarity in reasoning category distributions.

# Proposed First Experiments

Experiment 1 (Baseline): Set up experimental framework with a small public model. Replicate core measurement to establish baseline. ~8 hours.
Experiment 2 (Core): Apply methodology to target scenario. Measure primary metric vs baseline. ~12 hours.
Experiment 3 (Robustness): Test sensitivity to key parameters with 2+ variations. ~10 hours.

# Theory of Impact Chain

Homogeneous refusal reasoning limits the range of ethical considerations that AI systems apply, potentially systematically ignoring legitimate harm categories that require different justificatory frameworks.

# Strength Rationale

theory_of_impact (4/5): Strong chain: homogeneous refusal reasoning means AI systems systematically ignore certain ethical frameworks. Directly relevant to value alignment and safety boundary design.

accessible_complexity (4/5): Accessible with guidance: querying models via API with harmful prompts, extracting and categorizing refusal reasons, and computing similarity. Category coding is straightforward.

narrow_scope (4/5): Focused deliverable: 15+ models, 500 scenarios, categorize refusal types, compute inter-model similarity. Well-defined methodology with clear output table.

novelty (3/5): The existence of alignment homogeneity in refusal criteria and refusal prefixes is well-documented. Recent work explicitly shows overlapping risk categories and consistent refusal patterns across models. However, no existing work systematically categorizes the *reasoning* (ethical frameworks, justifications) behind refusals across many models at scale. The focus has been on whether models refuse, not on the diversity of reasons they cite. This specific angle is partially but not fully addressed.
